\title{Modelling TCP and TLS Latency Thresholds for Post-Quantum Authentication in Financial Messaging}

\begin{abstract}
Post-quantum authentication enlarges certificate chains and signed application objects, but its transport cost depends on how TLS writes interact with the TCP congestion window, not on byte count alone. We develop a per-segment model that combines OpenSSL's 4\,096-byte handshake-write pattern with Linux slow-start window validation to predict which authentication flights incur additional round trips. We evaluate it on full TLS~1.3 mutual-authentication transactions over an emulated IPv6 link (Linux, OpenSSL). Across 226 configurations, the model predicts excess latency with a mean absolute error (MAE) of 1.65\ms, against 4.70\ms for an idealised model, and errs by more than 0.4 RTT in 4 configurations (idealised: 21); 144 further configurations, measured later and not used to identify the mechanisms, give 2.04\ms. Tests on two additional machines reproduce round-trip-scale steps, but machine- and session-dependent offsets of up to 2.5\ms limit absolute accuracy. Validation covers ISO~8583-sized requests and one SWIFT-sized exchange; six suites have TLS measurements, while the other suites and protocol profiles are model applications. The validated MTUs are 1\,280 and 1\,500 bytes; 9\,000-byte full-handshake tests reveal unresolved flight-size errors. In a modelled SWIFT~MX scenario without session tickets, raising both endpoint ML-DSA-65 chain depths from 1 to 4 adds 60.0\ms at a 30\ms RTT, almost all of it slow-start waiting. With primitive costs measured on a Cortex-X925 core (OpenSSL~3.5.4) and no middlebox delay, transport overhead exceeds modelled primitive time in 75.6\% of the equally weighted ML-DSA-65 cold-connection grid. The flight that crosses the window should be located before deciding which bytes to remove.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Picture a payment gateway that uses mutual Transport Layer Security (mTLS). Every time a connection opens, the two sides trade certificate chains, and each payment request and response may carry a signature on top. Moving to post-quantum cryptography (PQC) makes all of these objects larger, at the transport layer and at the application layer alike: a signature of the Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (ML-DSA) at the ML-DSA-65 parameter set occupies 3\,309\,bytes~\cite{fips204}, whereas an Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA) P-256 signature occupies at most 72\,bytes in Distinguished Encoding Rules (DER) encoding~\cite{fips186}. What this costs in time, however, cannot be read off the sizes. A \emph{flight} is a group of messages that one endpoint sends before it has to wait for its peer. If a flight is longer than the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) congestion window allows, the sender pauses for acknowledgements (ACKs), and the pause lasts one round-trip time (RTT). Whether a flight crosses the window is therefore not a function of its size alone. \Cref{fig:walk} lets the reader watch this happen in one configuration that the testbed validates.

We shall keep coming back to the transaction of \cref{fig:walk}, and it helps to give it a face. A card terminal at a shop till sends an authorisation request (ISO~8583, message 0200) to the acquirer's gateway, and we imagine the cashier waiting for the answer (0210) before handing over the receipt. The request is 302\,bytes and the response 222: next to a web page, almost nothing. The two sides authenticate each other with certificates, and the public-key infrastructure behind them is an ordinary one, with an offline root, an issuing authority and an intermediate in between; the root is not sent, so each side sends three certificates. No real institution stands behind this call: the certificates are synthetic and the link is an emulated Internet Protocol version~6 (IPv6) link, but the sizes are those of real objects and the software is the real OpenSSL. Over a path with a 30\ms RTT, the classical version of this call took 94\ms from the first TCP SYN to the last byte of the response, in the median of 30 runs; with ML-DSA-65 signatures it took 157\ms. The payment, the gateway and the network were the same; only the signatures changed. The larger objects make two flights, the server's certificate flight and the client's reply, exceed the initial congestion window of ten segments, and each pays an extra round trip while it waits for acknowledgements. We call this the \emph{card-authorisation example}: it illustrates that even a small transaction can wait for extra round trips when its authentication data crosses a transport-layer threshold.

The reason for the move is well known. Rivest--Shamir--Adleman (RSA) and elliptic-curve cryptography would not survive a sufficiently large quantum computer~\cite{shor1994}, and for confidentiality the clock is set by how long recorded information must stay secret~\cite{mosca2018,baradziej2026}. In 2024 the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) standardised the Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism (ML-KEM), ML-DSA and the Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (SLH-DSA)~\cite{fips203,fips204,fips205}. Quantum-safe network protocols and infrastructure are now under assessment~\cite{baseri2024,baseri2026}, and central-bank pilots have already run post-quantum tunnels and signed financial messages~\cite{bis_leap2023,bis_leap2025}. What is still missing is a way to tell, before deployment, what an authenticated transaction will cost at the transport layer. Anyone choosing certificate chains and message-signing profiles needs that number. We take it as a working assumption, not as a finding of this study, that an additional round trip matters in financial messaging because it lengthens the time that a customer or a counterparty waits for an answer; how much it matters depends on the application, and we did not test it.

The usual answer is to estimate that cost from object sizes, the maximum segment size and the initial congestion window. Measurement studies report the slowdown for given certificate sizes and windows~\cite{sikeridis2020,paquin2020,kampanakis2024}, and size analyses compare the bytes of a flight with the window~\cite{kampanakis2022}, which amounts to rounding the size up to whole segments. Recent evaluation frameworks, transport-threshold experiments and segment-count captures widen the evidence across schemes, network conditions and TLS implementations~\cite{montenegro2026,montenegro2026quic,zhao2026,dubetskyy2026}, and chains and initial windows have been studied, though not together~\cite{henrich2025}. We found no earlier study that predicts which flight incurs an additional RTT on a specified implementation (\cref{sec:related}). The financial pilots cited above measure tunnel setup and message signing, but they do not take the latency of mTLS chains and application signatures apart; and the signed objects of the application layer, Extensible Markup Language Signature (XMLDSig), JSON Web Signature (JWS) and Demonstrating Proof of Possession (DPoP), add bytes above TLS that handshake-only analyses leave out.

Two behaviours of the tested stack decide where a flight waits. First, OpenSSL writes handshake data through a 4\,096-byte buffer and writes the application request separately, so the library, and not the message, fixes the boundaries at which TCP cuts segments~\cite{sosnowski2023}. Secondly, Linux validates window growth before it enlarges the window: after a short flight the window does not grow as a simplified model that adds every segment would predict~\cite{linuxtcp}. Neither behaviour suffices alone, since each removes only part of the error of a model that ignores both (\cref{sec:mech}, \cref{S-tab:ablation}); together they decide whether a later flight leaves without waiting for acknowledgements.

This paper builds a per-segment transport model that links the sizes of certificates, signatures and signed objects to the writes of the TLS library, the segmentation of TCP and the ACK-clocked growth of the congestion window, and so identifies the flights that exceed the window and the waiting that follows. By transport-layer latency we mean the time that TCP, and the way the TLS library writes into it, add to a transaction; the model splits the latency of a transaction into cryptographic computation, serialisation, propagation, slow-start waiting and an optional middlebox delay. We keep the scope deliberately narrow: new connections with a full TLS~1.3 handshake over a wired, emulated IPv6 link, with Linux and OpenSSL as the reference implementation, no new cryptographic primitive and no security proof. Session resumption and the session ticket change the window that the server has for its response; their mechanism is studied in follow-up work whose measurements are in the archive~\cite{dinh2026artifact}, and the results here apply to full handshakes without tickets. We chose Linux/OpenSSL because it is open and can be measured to the last byte, not because we know a particular bank gateway to run on it; the write pattern of the library and the window rule of the kernel are inputs that can be swapped.

The study makes three contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{A mechanism-based predictive model.} A per-segment model that integrates the OpenSSL write pattern with Linux-style slow-start window validation predicts which flights cross the window and the round-trip-scale latency steps that follow. Unlike approaches based on byte counts, it accounts for the implementation-specific behaviour that decides where each flight waits, and it carries the certificate chains of both peers and the application-layer signatures of a transaction.
\item \emph{A validation that states where the model works and where it does not.} Against real OpenSSL~3.5 transactions on 226 configurations, the mean absolute error (MAE) of configuration-level excess latency is 1.65\ms, where an idealised closed-form model gives 4.70\ms, with errors of whole round trips; 144 further configurations, measured later and not used to identify the mechanisms, give 2.04\ms. On a second x86 machine and an Arm machine the round-trip steps reproduce and the window rule holds on four Linux kernels, while absolute latencies differ by up to 2.5\ms between machines and sessions. Pre-registered tests, whose failures are reported, mark the limits of the model.
\item \emph{Implications for the design of financial transactions.} The model quantifies what certificate-chain depth, certificate embedding, application signatures, connection reuse and certificate compression do to the latency of thirteen suites and six financial and application programming interface (API) profiles, and how far each effect depends on the initial window and on the assumed processing cost. Six suites have full TLS transaction measurements; the other suites and the profiles are model applications, labelled as such.
\end{itemize}

The evidence comes from synthetic bank-like certificates, measured cryptographic operations and request--response probes on a kernel-emulated wired link. The validated domain covers maximum transmission units (MTUs) of 1\,280 and 1\,500 bytes; at 9\,000 bytes the pre-registered full-handshake tests leave flight-size errors unresolved. The main contribution of the model is therefore its ability to locate transport-induced, round-trip-scale latency steps under the evaluated Linux/OpenSSL conditions, not to estimate absolute latency to the millisecond. \Cref{sec:limits} lists in full what was and was not tested.

\Cref{sec:related} reviews prior work and states the gap. \Cref{sec:method} walks through one transaction flight by flight and brings in the objects, the bytes and the per-segment model where each is needed. Identification and validation follow in \cref{sec:testbed}; \cref{sec:robust} then pushes the model into other settings and says where it breaks. With the instrument tested, \cref{sec:results} asks four questions a designer is likely to ask. \Cref{sec:discussion} gathers the evidence levels and the implications, and \cref{sec:conclusion} closes.

\subsection{Measurement studies of post-quantum TLS}
\label{sec:related-meas}
Most of what we know about the cost of post-quantum authentication in network protocols comes from measurement, and it started with a simple suspicion: size, not computation, is the obstacle. Kampanakis et~al.\ analysed how post-quantum X.509 certificates fragment in TLS and IKEv2 and tested the analysis experimentally~\cite{kampanakis2018}. Sikeridis et~al.\ then measured TLS~1.3 with several signature candidates and certificate-chain compositions~\cite{sikeridis2020}, and in a study of TLS and Secure Shell (SSH) found that a larger initial window reduces the post-quantum slowdown~\cite{sikeridis2020b}. Paquin et~al.\ evaluated latency and packet loss on a Linux-emulated network~\cite{paquin2020}. Henrich et~al.\ extended that framework to post-quantum and hybrid key-encapsulation mechanisms under delay, loss and transmission-rate limits, with ECDSA authentication; they noted that a larger message costs an extra round trip only when its segments exceed the congestion window, but they did not predict where that happens~\cite{henrich2023}.

A second group of studies widens the evidence beyond one library or one transport. Montenegro et~al.\ built a containerised OpenSSL framework and counted TLS handshakes per 10\,s and bytes per handshake for classical, hybrid and post-quantum key exchange and signatures, with mutual authentication, delays of 1, 10 and 100\ms and packet loss of 0.1--1\%; their certificate chain has two certificates of one algorithm, and other trust-chain configurations are named as future work~\cite{montenegro2026}. A later study of theirs compared TLS over TCP with QUIC under added delay and packet loss of 5 to 20\,\%, varying the key exchange with one classical signature fixed at each security level, and found that QUIC reduces the post-quantum penalty, in particular under loss~\cite{montenegro2026quic}. R{\'\i}os and Montenegro measured TLS handshakes with mutual authentication and ML-DSA on two platforms and split the handshake time into cryptographic and other time~\cite{rios2026}. Raavi et~al.\ compared QUIC with TCP/TLS under packet drop with post-quantum signatures in a single certificate~\cite{raavi2022quic}, and later captured the TCP packetisation of such handshakes on local virtual machines~\cite{raavi2025}.

A third group looks at authentication in particular settings. Paul et~al.\ proposed mixed certificate chains, with hash-based signatures only at the root and ECDSA or lattice signatures below, and measured them with mutual authentication on cloud servers at three distances; the paths were not controlled and no transport model was offered~\cite{paul2022}. B{\"u}rstinghaus-Steinbach et~al.\ showed that hash-based signatures are costly on embedded servers~\cite{burstinghaus2020}. Akrom et~al.\ benchmarked RSA-2048, ECC-P256, ML-KEM-512, ML-DSA-44 and Falcon-512 on an Arm platform, from primitives to TLS handshakes, low bandwidth and concurrent connections, to rank the algorithms for migration planning at security level~1; explaining handshake latency was not their aim~\cite{akrom2027}. A survey of post-quantum implementations compiles primitive benchmarks across CPUs, GPUs, FPGAs and microcontrollers and lists communication overhead among the deployment trade-offs, without measuring a protocol~\cite{sakwa2026}. At the far end of the bandwidth scale, Ahn et~al.\ sent a Kyber512 and Dilithium2 TLS~1.3 handshake of 7\,675 bytes over an acoustic channel in a sea trial; the ServerHello, which carries the signature material, was the largest flight and dominated the session time, but the channel replaces TCP there, so no window mechanism is involved~\cite{ahn2026}.

\subsection{Studies that relate the handshake to the transport}
\label{sec:related-near}
A smaller set of studies comes closer to our question, because it relates the size of the handshake to the behaviour of the transport. We take them in turn, and say for each what it establishes and what it leaves open.

\emph{OpenSSL write buffer and initial window.} Sosnowski et~al.\ combined black- and white-box measurements of post-quantum TLS~1.3 under emulated loss, bandwidth and delay, and observed that the internal buffer of OpenSSL decides when the ServerHello leaves~\cite{sosnowski2023}; they did not use this to predict segments or round trips. Henrich et~al.\ added signature schemes, chains of one to three certificates with mixed algorithms on real servers in several countries, and an initial-window sweep for key exchange~\cite{henrich2025}. Data beyond the window adds a round trip, they report, and a larger window removes it. Chains and windows are not combined, though, mutual authentication is not varied, and a predictive model, together with the thresholds at which round trips appear, is left as future work. That last item is where the present study starts.

\emph{MTU thresholds.} Zhao scanned adjacent MTU values to find where the ClientHello and ServerHello change their segment count, and injected loss in each direction~\cite{zhao2026}. The study covers key exchange only, with one self-signed RSA certificate, and varies neither certificate chains, nor application signatures, nor the write pattern of the library.

\emph{From the handshake to the last byte.} Kampanakis and Childs-Klein~\cite{kampanakis2024} moved the metric from the handshake to the time to last byte, with \texttt{s2n-tls} in namespaces joined by virtual Ethernet pairs under \texttt{tc netem}. With an initial window of 10 segments a server chain of 16\,KB adds one round trip to the handshake, an initial window of 20 removes it, and the extra round trip is amortised as the response grows towards 200\,KiB, because the large handshake flight lets the window grow early; under packet loss the delay that the network itself adds is larger than the cost of the bigger handshake. Their chains are simulated RSA chains of 2.5, 8 and 16\,KB on the server side, without mutual authentication, and the authors do not predict which flight pays the round trip: ``depending on how the TCP window aligns with the data size'' the classical connection may or may not be one round trip faster. That alignment is what this paper turns into a prediction. The two results are compatible: their amortisation concerns responses of tens to hundreds of kilobytes, whereas the transactions here are small (a response of at most 3.6\,KiB), where there is nothing to amortise it over.

\emph{Segment counts and loss tails.} Dubetskyy and Cano~\cite{dubetskyy2026} compare OpenSSL~3.4.1 with \texttt{oqs-provider} and OpenSSL~3.5.0 on two x86 hosts, time the HTTPS request to the first byte over loopback and over a virtual Ethernet pair with \texttt{tc netem} (delay 0 to 100\ms, loss 0 to 2\,\%, MTU 1\,280 to 1\,500 bytes), and capture the TCP segments of the handshake. The server sends 4 or 5 payload segments with RSA-PSS and 10 to 13 with ML-DSA-65, and the tail under loss consists mostly of delays of about one second, which they relate to lost connection-establishment segments. Their study is descriptive: one self-signed certificate and no chain, one run per condition for most conditions, and no link between the segment counts and the congestion window, so it does not say which flight pays a round trip. The two studies agree where they overlap: the 15 server segment counts that they captured (five combinations of key exchange and certificate, three MTUs) all follow from the 4\,096-byte write pattern of our model (\cref{sec:mech}).

\subsection{Shrinking the authentication data}
\label{sec:related-shrink}
A second line of work tries to shrink the problem. Field measurements with enlarged certificate chains show that the initial window sets the handshake slowdown and that some clients or middleboxes fail at large handshake sizes~\cite{westerbaan2021}. Suppressing intermediate certificates saves authentication bytes~\cite{kampanakis2022}; key-based authentication replaces handshake signatures~\cite{schwabe2020}; and Merkle Tree Certificates swap per-certificate signatures for inclusion proofs~\cite{mtcdraft}. All of these assume that fewer bytes mean lower latency. Yet a saved byte removes a round trip only when it brings a flight under a transport threshold, and no one has located that threshold for certificate-based TLS and application objects.

\subsection{Financial pilots and the policy work around them}
\label{sec:related-field}
Project Leap Phase~1 measured post-quantum IPsec tunnel establishment between central banks~\cite{bis_leap2023}. Phase~2 evaluated post-quantum signing of ISO~20022 messages in a payment-system test environment, with software keys and raw public keys, and reported receiver-side verification times of 209.9\ms for round-3 Dilithium category~3 and 28.1\ms for RSA~\cite{bis_leap2025}. The Banque de France trial deployed a post-quantum IPsec VPN with hardware security modules and Dilithium certificates, aimed at interoperability and crypto-agility; tunnel set-up took 4 to 5\,s with FrodoKEM level~5, and the report itself says that the result would have to be re-examined for a TLS connection~\cite{bdf2022}. The BdF-MAS trial demonstrated hybrid signed and encrypted e-mail between two central banks~\cite{bdfmas2024}. Around these pilots sits a body of survey and policy work. Surveys of quantum-safe network protocols and infrastructure identify migration and interoperability risk~\cite{baseri2024,baseri2026}. The harvest-now-decrypt-later threat sets the schedule for data that must stay confidential~\cite{baradziej2026}. A survey of post-quantum TLS classifies the proposals and reports that size matters more the worse the network, and speed more the better it is, without a transport model~\cite{alnahawi2024}. Supervisory and regulatory documents treat the migration as a matter of planning, cryptographic inventory and operational resilience~\cite{g7qtwg2026,andriani2024,dora2022,janciute2025}. What these works examine is integration, tunnel set-up, risk and governance. None of them measures certificate chains at more than one depth, and none takes apart the latency overhead of mTLS chains and application signatures in payment protocols.

\subsection{What this paper adds}
\label{sec:related-gap}
Taken together, these studies establish the effect of authentication size, network conditions and window settings. Their unit of analysis, however, is the byte count, the packet capture or the end-to-end time, and their chains are fixed or have a single level. To our knowledge, no study combines the write pattern of the TLS library with kernel window growth to predict which flight crosses a window threshold on a specified implementation, and validates the prediction against real transactions. Against the closest studies of \cref{sec:related-near}, this paper adds five things.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{A prediction.} It predicts the round trips from a model of two stack behaviours and tests the prediction on 226 configurations, where the closest studies describe or measure.
\item \emph{Chains in both directions.} It varies the certificate chain from one to four certificates at both endpoints under mutual authentication, which is where flights cross the window; the closest studies use a single certificate or a server chain.
\item \emph{A mechanism.} It identifies the window rule on plain TCP and on four kernels, and the write pattern of OpenSSL, instead of observing the effect.
\item \emph{A validation that reports its failures.} It repeats the validation on three machines and reports the pre-registered tests that the model did not pass.
\item \emph{The application layer.} It adds the signed objects of the financial protocol profiles, as a model application, so that the transport cost of the chain and of the application signatures can be compared.
\end{itemize}
It leaves out what several of those studies make their subject: packet loss (only a small test is reported, in Supplementary Appendix~\ref{S-app:loss}), QUIC, other TLS libraries and traffic over the wide area.


\section{One transaction, flight by flight}
\label{sec:method}

Follow a transaction between a payment client and a gateway from start to end and it consists of a TCP connection, a TLS~1.3 handshake and one or more request-response exchanges. The model predicts how long all this takes from the authentication objects and the parameters of the link. \Cref{fig:pipeline} summarises the pipeline. A flow builder converts a suite, a protocol profile, a chain depth, $d$, and a connection mode into an ordered sequence of central processing unit (CPU) steps and byte transfers. A cold transaction opens a new TCP and TLS connection; a warm transaction reuses an established connection. A replay engine executes the flow over a wired link. In \cref{fig:pipeline}, $\pi$ and $\theta$ denote the optional middlebox delay and its segment threshold (\cref{sec:transport}). \Cref{S-tab:protocols,S-tab:constants} in Supplementary Appendix~\ref{S-app:inputs} list the parameters; the detailed tables and derivations (Appendices SA--SL) are in the Supplementary Material, and the code and data are public (see Data availability).

\subsection{Cryptographic suites and protocol profiles}
A suite pairs a key-establishment mechanism, a key exchange or a key-encapsulation mechanism (KEM), with one signature scheme. The signature scheme signs the certificate chain, the TLS \texttt{CertificateVerify} message and the application objects. \Cref{tab:algos} gives, for the suites measured in TLS and for Falcon-512, the public-key (PK), SubjectPublicKeyInfo (SPKI) and signature sizes in bytes and the median times on one Cortex-X925 core with OpenSSL~3.5.4 (liboqs~0.14.0 for Falcon); \cref{S-tab:algos_full} has all thirteen suites. Its key-establishment rows give the TLS key-share sizes and the client and server computation. The classical baseline is ECDSA-P256 with the X25519 elliptic-curve Diffie--Hellman function; RSA-2048 and RSA-3072 with Elliptic Curve Diffie--Hellman Ephemeral (ECDHE) key exchange complete the classical suites. ECDSA-P256 with the hybrid X25519MLKEM768 key share migrates only the key establishment. The post-quantum suites pair ML-KEM with ML-DSA-44/65/87, with Falcon-512/1024~\cite{falcon2020} or with SLH-DSA-128s/128f. The composite suites use ML-DSA-44/65 + ECDSA-P256 certificates~\cite{compositedraft} with the hybrid key share of RFC~10024~\cite{hybriddraft}. The composite specification was an Internet-Draft in the RFC Editor queue at the time of writing. Sizes follow the cited specifications. Because the suites span different NIST security categories, ranking them by performance is not an equal-security comparison. Composite sizes and CPU costs are the sums of the constituent schemes, since we did not measure interoperable composite objects. We measured the CPU costs (\cref{sec:meas}). The sweeps scale them by 2 (the measured efficiency core), by 4 (close to the measured x86 core) and by 16 (an assumed slow-processing case). The factor 16 is not a measurement of a hardware security module (HSM).

The six profiles are ISO~8583 0200/0210 card messages protected by symmetric message authentication codes (MACs)~\cite{iso8583}; ISO~20022 pacs.008/pacs.002 payment messages~\cite{iso20022} and SWIFT~MX messages, each signed with XMLDSig~\cite{xmldsig} over mTLS; OAuth~2.0 with bearer tokens~\cite{rfc6749,rfc6750}; OpenID Connect (OIDC)~\cite{oidccore}; and Financial-grade API (FAPI)~2.0~\cite{fapi2} with signed request objects, DPoP~\cite{rfc9449} and mTLS~\cite{rfc8705}. SWIFT~MX denotes the ISO~20022-based message format of the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication (SWIFT). The profiles are representative scenario choices, not mandatory combinations of security mechanisms; the selected FAPI profile, for instance, combines DPoP and mTLS, and the XMLDSig profiles embed the signer certificate by default. Message sizes exclude the algorithm-dependent bytes, which the model adds according to each profile: encoded signatures, embedded public keys and signer certificates. \Cref{sec:sens} evaluates a reference to the XML signer certificate in place of embedding.

\subsection{Authentication objects and transaction flow}
The size of a modelled certificate controls the bytes of each authentication flight. A certificate $i$ has length
\begin{equation}
L_i=m_i+b_\mathrm{SPKI}+b_\sigma+4,
\label{eq:cert}
\end{equation}
where $L_i$ is the encoded length in bytes, $m_i$ is the algorithm-independent metadata length, $b_\mathrm{SPKI}$ is the length of the SPKI field, $b_\sigma$ is the signature length and 4\,bytes is the outer DER framing. \Cref{eq:cert} separates fixed metadata from the algorithm-dependent key and signature fields. We set $m_i=900$\,bytes for a leaf and $m_i=520$\,bytes for a certification authority (CA) certificate (empirical choice; \cref{sec:sens} varies the two values). A transmitted chain of depth $d$ contains the leaf and $d-1$ intermediates and excludes the root~\cite{rfc5280}. Under mTLS, the same $d$ applies to the two endpoint chains in the main sweep; \cref{S-tab:asym} gives them separate depths. An increase of $d$ by one adds one transmitted intermediate at each endpoint.

A cold transaction opens a new TCP connection and performs a full TLS~1.3 handshake~\cite{rfc8446}. The ordered transfers are the ClientHello, the server authentication flight, the client authentication flight, a separately written first request and the remaining request--response exchanges. The model charges key establishment, handshake signatures, $d+1$ verification operations per authenticated chain (with \texttt{CertificateVerify}) and all application signatures. These CPU steps are serial on the modelled critical path. The model does not overlap certificate processing with the reception of the remaining flight bytes.

\subsection{Where the bytes go: authentication-byte composition}
This analysis locates the bytes that post-quantum authentication adds, for a cold transaction at each protocol's reference depth. ML-DSA-65 with ML-KEM-768 enlarges a cold SWIFT~MX transaction from 23.0 to 83.4\,KiB. Falcon-512 yields 39.9\,KiB and SLH-DSA-128f yields 246.7\,KiB. \Cref{S-fig:sizes} shows the composition by suite for SWIFT~MX at depth~3 and FAPI~2.0 at depth~2. For ML-DSA-65, certificate chains account for 42\% of the bytes, XMLDSig signatures and embedded certificates for 30\%, \texttt{CertificateVerify} for 8\% and the payment payload for 17\%; key shares and framing make up the other 3\%. The leanest protocols grow the most: ISO~8583 grows by a factor of 6.8 with ML-DSA-65, because its handshake chains dwarf a 300-byte authorisation, while ISO~20022 grows 4.4 times and FAPI~2.0 5.7 times (\cref{S-tab:sizes}). The hybrid key share adds 2.2\,KiB. On a warm connection ISO~8583 does not grow at all, since it uses a symmetric MAC; ISO~20022 and FAPI~2.0 still grow 3.1 and 4.8 times, because their application-layer signatures remain.


\subsection{Where a flight waits: the per-segment transport model}
\label{sec:transport}
The reference path uses IPv6, so the MTU and the maximum segment size (MSS) satisfy $\mathrm{MSS}=\mathrm{MTU}-60$\,bytes, and each direction keeps its own congestion window. The initial window is $c_0=10$ segments~\cite{rfc6928,rfc5681}. A warm transaction reuses an established TLS connection without a handshake; it is not TLS session resumption, which opens a new connection with a ticket or a pre-shared key and is outside the model. Its reference window restarts at $c_0$, which represents an idle connection; a sensitivity case retains a 64-segment window, which represents a busy one.

The ACK clock sets when each segment of a flight leaves the sender. Consider a flight of $N$ segments on a first-in, first-out (FIFO) link. The segment release time $r_k$, start time $s_k$, departure time $\ell_k$ and ACK return time $a_k$ follow
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
r_k&=\begin{cases}0,& k<c,\\a_{\lfloor(k-c)/2\rfloor},&k\ge c,\end{cases}\\
s_k&=\max(\ell_{k-1},r_k),\quad
\ell_k=s_k+\tau_k,\quad a_k=\ell_k+\mathrm{RTT},
\end{split}
\label{eq:flight}
\end{equation}
where $k=0,\dots,N-1$ is the zero-based segment index, $c$ is the window at the start of the flight, $\tau_k$ is the serialisation time of segment $k$ and $\ell_{-1}=0$. The floor operator $\lfloor\cdot\rfloor$ releases two queued segments per returned ACK in slow start. \Cref{eq:flight} controls the slow-start waiting inside a flight. The last byte arrives at $\ell_{N-1}+\mathrm{RTT}/2$ under a symmetric one-way delay. The recurrence costs $\mathcal{O}(N)$ operations per flight. The model excludes ACK serialisation, receive-window limits, packet loss and queueing from other flows.

The per-segment model has two variants, the stack model and the idealised model. The stack model represents two observed behaviours. First, the tested OpenSSL configuration writes handshake data through a 4\,096-byte buffer and writes the application request separately. With \texttt{TCP\_NODELAY}, a partial segment at a write boundary leaves the sender when the window permits it. The model accounts for these boundaries and coalesces queued tails when the window is exhausted. Second, the model updates the next flight window using Linux-style slow-start validation. In the inspected kernel headers, \texttt{tcp\_is\_cwnd\_limited()} first accepts an explicitly window-limited sender. In slow start, it then tests whether \texttt{snd\_cwnd} is below twice \texttt{max\_packets\_out}, the tracked maximum of outstanding packets~\cite{linuxtcp}. The test uses this tracked maximum, not the instantaneous number of packets in flight. The model tracks the peak number of outstanding segments during ACK replay and updates the window between flights using this value. A short flight that uses less than half of the available window does not enlarge it in this abstraction. The source inspection covers Linux~6.11 and 6.14. We empirically examined the behaviour of the testbed kernel (Linux~7.0) and of three other kernels (6.8, 6.14, 6.17; \cref{sec:repl}); \cref{sec:mech} reports a plain-TCP measurement of the window on the testbed kernel. RFC~7661 specifies a related congestion-window validation method for rate-limited traffic~\cite{rfc7661}. The model follows the inspected Linux rule and does not implement RFC~7661.

The idealised model is the baseline of this study: it uses $\lceil\text{bytes}/\mathrm{MSS}\rceil$ segments per flight, where $\lceil\cdot\rceil$ is the ceiling operator, and it enlarges the window by the segment count after each flight. The stack model keeps the ACK-clock recurrence of \cref{eq:flight} within each flight. It is an abstraction of the observed stack, not an execution of the complete Linux TCP state machine.

The model decomposes the latency of a transaction as
\begin{equation}
T=T_\cpu+T_\mathrm{ser}+T_\mathrm{prop}+T_\mathrm{ssw}+T_\mathrm{pen},
\label{eq:latency}
\end{equation}
where $T_\cpu$ is cryptographic computation, $T_\mathrm{ser}$ is serialisation, $T_\mathrm{prop}$ is propagation, $T_\mathrm{ssw}$ is slow-start waiting and $T_\mathrm{pen}$ is an optional added middlebox delay. \Cref{eq:latency} is exact for the serial model. Real processing can overlap network reception. The size-dependent transport overhead is
\begin{equation}
O=T_\mathrm{ser}+T_\mathrm{ssw}+T_\mathrm{pen}.
\label{eq:overhead}
\end{equation}
\Cref{eq:overhead} excludes $T_\mathrm{prop}$, since propagation is common to the compared suites. The comparison of $O$ with $T_\cpu$ states whether a transaction is transport-bound or computation-bound.

The optional delay term is $T_\mathrm{pen}=\pi\,\bigl|\{f:N_f>\theta\}\bigr|$, where $N_f$ is the segment count of flight $f$, $|\cdot|$ denotes set cardinality, $\pi$ is the added delay in milliseconds and $\theta$ is the segment threshold. The term represents a hypothetical size-threshold delay rather than a measured law of middlebox processing. Its reference value is $\pi=0$, and larger values serve only as sensitivity inputs. Reported connection failures with large handshakes~\cite{westerbaan2021} motivate the examination of size-related constraints. They do not calibrate $\pi$, and the model does not represent connection failures. Size outputs use KiB ($1\,\mathrm{KiB}=1024$\,bytes). Bit rates use decimal Mbit/s.

For certificate compression~\cite{rfc8879}, the model treats keys and signatures as incompressible. The compression ratio of DER metadata is 0.68 for the first certificate and 0.52 for each subsequent certificate (calibrated on five public bank and payment chains). The assumed client decompression rates are 330, 33, 3.3 and 0.33\,MB/s. These rates are sensitivity inputs, not measurements of embedded clients.

\paragraph{The card-authorisation example, flight by flight} Take the transaction of \cref{fig:walk} and read its flights off the model. The ClientHello is a single segment. With ECDSA-P256 the server answers with a flight of three segments and the client replies with three more (its certificates and, written separately, the request), so every flight fits in the window of ten, and the call costs three round trips of 30\ms in all: the TCP handshake and two exchanges. With ML-DSA-65 the server's flight swells to 16 segments and so does the client's. Each sends ten, then waits for the acknowledgements that let it send the rest; the model gives these two flights 45.8 and 45.7\ms where an unobstructed flight takes 15. Nothing was misconfigured. The certificates had simply grown past what the window could carry at once, and a call that took three round trips now took five. \Cref{fig:ladder} draws the server's flight in both cases.

\section{Identifying the mechanisms and validating the model}
\label{sec:testbed}

A model has to answer to real transactions, so this section puts ours to the test. It first sets out what goes in: the parameter space, the measured inputs and the checks on the implementation. On a kernel-emulated testbed it then identifies the two stack behaviours and measures how far, and where, the predictions miss. \Cref{sec:robust} takes the model into other network and TCP settings and ends with the places where it fails or has not been tried.

\subsection{Parameter space and reported quantities}
The main sweep contains 140\,400 cold-connection rows and 35\,100 warm-connection rows, for a total of 175\,500 rows. The cold rows form the full factorial of thirteen suites, six profiles, four chain depths, six bit rates (10, 100, 200, 400, 800 and 1\,600\Mbits), five RTTs (5, 12, 30, 50 and 100\ms), three MTUs (1\,280, 1\,500 and 9\,000\,bytes) and five assumed delays $\pi\in\{0,3,6,12,24\}$\ms. Warm rows transmit no chain and have a single depth state. The threshold is $\theta=4$ segments; a separate sweep uses $\theta\in\{2,4,8,16\}$.

Unless stated otherwise, individual predictions use 100\Mbits, a 30\ms RTT, an MTU of 1\,500\,bytes, $\pi=0$ and a cold connection. The baseline suite is ECDSA-P256 with X25519. The share of transport-bound rows is
\begin{equation}
\phi=\frac{\bigl|\{j:O_j>T_{\cpu,j}\}\bigr|}{J},
\label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
where $j$ indexes the $J$ equally weighted cold rows of the selected grid. $\phi$ is a descriptive fraction of the chosen grid, not an estimate of the probability of bank traffic. \Cref{S-tab:protocols} lists the protocol reference depths and fixed object sizes.

\subsection{Measured inputs}
\label{sec:meas}
\paragraph{Primitive costs} The Arm primitive benchmarks and object-size measurements ran on an ASUS Ascent GX10 with an NVIDIA GB10 (10 Cortex-X925 and 10 Cortex-A725 cores; Linux~6.14), called the GX10 below. A C harness measured per-operation costs through the high-level envelope (EVP) interface of OpenSSL~3.5.4~\cite{openssl35} for RSA with probabilistic signature scheme (RSA-PSS) padding, ECDSA, X25519, P-256, ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA-SHA2, and liboqs~0.14.0~\cite{stebila2016} provided Falcon. Each value is the median of seven 0.5\,s runs pinned to one core; the minimum and maximum lie within 0.6\% of the median for the median operation and within 6\% for the noisiest one, ML-DSA-87 signing (\cref{S-tab:cpu}). ML-DSA-65 signing costs 0.59\ms with OpenSSL (0.27\ms with liboqs) and SLH-DSA-128s signing 171\ms; the efficiency core is 1.5 to 2.4 times slower than the performance core.

To anchor the CPU scale on a second architecture we repeated the measurement on one core of an Intel Core i7-5600U (Broadwell, 2.6\,GHz, AVX2, turbo disabled), the machine that hosts the testbed of \cref{sec:testbed}. Elliptic-curve and lattice operations run 2.2 to 4.4 times slower than on the X925 (median 3.15 for signatures and 3.87 for key establishment), RSA signing 1.7 times slower and the hash-based SLH-DSA about 8 times slower, since this core has no SHA instruction extensions (\cref{S-tab:cpux86}); two cores of an idle machine agree within 4\% for signatures. The CPU scale of 4 in the sweeps therefore corresponds to a measured x86 core; the scale of 16 is an assumed processing slowdown, not an HSM measurement.

\paragraph{Certificates, handshakes and application objects} We generated DER hierarchies with a root, 0--3 intermediates and server and client leaves with bank-like extensions for RSA, ECDSA, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA, and recorded 56 configurations of real TLS~1.3 handshakes with OpenSSL \texttt{s\_server}/\texttt{s\_client} (21 handshakes each, depth 1--4, with and without mTLS) through a loopback relay that counts the bytes of each flight. OpenSSL~3.5 has no TLS code points for SLH-DSA or Falcon, so for these schemes the certificates and the CPU costs are measured and the handshakes are modelled. We also produced real signed application objects with OpenSSL (JWS client assertions and access tokens, DPoP proofs with the key in the JSON Object Signing and Encryption (JOSE) header, and an XMLDSig signature over a pacs.008 document with the signer certificate in \texttt{KeyInfo}) and compared each with the model.

\subsection{Implementation verification and input checks}
The test suites check byte accounting, latency-component sums and selected monotonicity properties. An independently implemented SimPy discrete-event scheduler reproduces the transport outputs of 126\,360 flows with a maximum absolute latency difference of $1.3\times10^{-11}$\ms; the full event-driven grid uses the stack model, and an additional 20\,000-flow sample checks the idealised model. This verifies that two implementations of the same assumptions agree; it does not validate the assumptions against a physical network.

The input checks concern byte construction. With certificate metadata calibrated on the generated ECDSA chain (854 bytes per leaf and 663 per CA certificate; the sweeps use 900 and 520, within the range varied in \cref{sec:sens}), the predicted certificate sizes differ by at most 0.8\% (\cref{S-tab:certs}) and the modelled TLS flights from the OpenSSL handshakes by a median of 0.02\% and at most 0.40\% (\cref{S-tab:tls}). The model overstates the measured JWS and DPoP objects by 130 to 250 bytes (41--45\% for ECDSA-P256, 22--29\% for RSA-2048, at most 6.3\% for the post-quantum suites) and the XMLDSig document by at most 21 bytes (\cref{S-tab:app}). A check on two public servers found that X25519MLKEM768 adds 2.9 and 2.5\ms at RTTs of 30 and 34\ms, about a tenth of an RTT or less (\cref{S-tab:internet}); it is an input check, not a validation.

The loopback handshake-time residual after subtraction of the modelled primitive time is $-0.1$ to 1.1\ms at the 10th--90th percentiles. With the measured x86 costs, the modelled CPU steps differ from 24 unshaped transaction timings by an MAE of 1.52\ms (mean error, model minus measurement, $-1.14$\ms). These residuals contain library and local-stack work that primitive benchmarks do not capture; in 3 of the 14 handshakes of \cref{S-tab:tls} the modelled CPU time exceeds the measured loopback time (by up to 0.62\ms), consistent with a serial model that does not overlap operations.
\subsection{Identification and validation: design and testbed}
A client whose ML-DSA-65 authentication flight is one segment above the available window waits one additional RTT; to locate such waits the model must contain certain stack behaviours. We evaluate the identification grid and the later measurements at other bit rates, MTUs and initial windows separately.

\paragraph{Testbed}
We replayed real transactions over an emulated link, with a virtual Ethernet pair joining two Linux network namespaces. The \texttt{tc netem} network emulator adds a one-way delay of RTT/2 in each direction, a rate limit and the MTU. The link uses IPv6 with TCP timestamps disabled. The headers occupy the $40+20$\,bytes of the model ($\mathrm{MSS}=\mathrm{MTU}-60$\,bytes). We disable segmentation offload, so each segment is one packet. The sender uses Reno slow start with quick acknowledgements and a per-route initial window. This configuration is the closest Linux configuration to \cref{eq:flight}.

A C program built on OpenSSL~3.5 opens a new TCP connection and performs a full TLS~1.3 handshake with mutual authentication. It uses the real DER chains of \cref{sec:meas} without session tickets or a session cache. It sends a 302-byte request and receives a 222-byte response, the ISO~8583 authorisation profile. One experiment uses SWIFT-sized requests of 10.9\,KiB and 3.6\,KiB. Client and server run on separate physical cores of an Intel Core i7-5600U (two cores, four threads; Linux~7.0.0-42, iproute2~6.1, HAProxy~2.8.30). Supplementary Appendix~\ref{S-app:machines} describes the three machines, their kernels and the settings of the campaigns. The shaped measurements contain 20 repetitions in 144 configurations, 30 in 72 and 50--80 in ten after pooling; the runner discards three warm-up transactions. The CPU clock of the testbed host was fixed for the campaigns of the identification and validation sets: the runner recorded the machine state at the start of each run, and the snapshots of these campaigns show the \texttt{performance} governor and disabled turbo (\texttt{intel\_no\_turbo}\,=\,1), set with the repository script \texttt{host\_tune.sh}. The runs with other TCP settings (CUBIC, delayed acknowledgements, timestamps, BBR) were repeated with the same fixed clock; the first session (\texttt{ondemand} governor, turbo enabled; archived in the repository) gave MAEs within 0.08\ms for Reno and CUBIC and 5.47 against 3.51\ms for BBR. The kernel TCP counters (\texttt{TCP\_INFO}) report the segment size, the initial window and the number of data segments of each transaction.

\subsection{Metric definition}
The excess latency $E_j$ of configuration $j$ is the median time of the shaped configuration minus the median time of an unshaped run with the same suite, depth and authentication mode. The model prediction is $\hat{E}_j=T_j-T_{\cpu,j}$. The subtraction removes common primitive, library and local-stack costs, though only approximately, since scheduling and overlap can differ between shaped and unshaped runs. The error is $e_j=\hat{E}_j-E_j$ (model minus measurement). The accuracy metrics over $M$ configurations are
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}|e_j|,\qquad
\mathrm{RMSE}=\Bigl(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}e_j^2\Bigr)^{1/2},\qquad
\bar{e}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}e_j,
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
where RMSE is the root-mean-square error and $\bar{e}$ is the bias. Each error uses configuration-level medians.

\subsection{Comparison design and observed mechanisms}
\label{sec:mech}
The retained direct-path dataset contains 5\,660 successful shaped transactions and 1\,300 unshaped baselines, 6\,960 transactions in total. The comparison covers 226 configurations: six suites, chain depth 1--4, RTT of 5--100\ms, bit rates of 10, 100 and 1\,000\Mbits, MTUs of 1\,280, 1\,500 and 9\,000\,bytes and initial windows of 10 and 32 segments. The six suites use the real DER chains of \cref{sec:meas} with mutual authentication: RSA-2048 and ECDSA-P256 with classical key exchange; ML-DSA-44, ML-DSA-65 and ML-DSA-87 with ML-KEM; and ECDSA-P256 with the hybrid X25519MLKEM768 group. The hybrid group uses code point 0x11EC, registered by RFC~10024. OpenSSL~3.5.4 does not use the pre-standard Kyber code points. The ML-DSA suites use the pure groups MLKEM768 and MLKEM1024, which RFC~10024 does not define; an Internet-Draft in the RFC Editor queue defines them~\cite{tlsmlkem}. The ML-DSA signature schemes of the OpenSSL TLS stack follow an Internet-Draft~\cite{tlsmldsa}. Falcon-512, SLH-DSA and the composite suites cannot run in OpenSSL~3.5 TLS. Their results rest on the model and on measured certificate sizes and CPU costs. As for bytes, the TLS bytes read and written by the client lie within 0.95--1.09 times the modelled bytes of the whole transaction. The measured bytes exceed the modelled bytes by a median of 127\,bytes (maximum 763\,bytes), which we did not itemise, whereas the modelled handshake flights differ from the OpenSSL handshakes by at most 0.40\% (\cref{sec:meas}). Below, we check the segment count, which decides the round trips, directly against the kernel counters.

\Cref{fig:testbed,tab:testbed} compare the transport time. Panel (a) of \cref{fig:testbed} shows the idealised model, and panel (b) shows the stack model with the two mechanisms below. The idealised model has an MAE of 4.70\ms and a maximum error of 97.1\ms. In 21 configurations its error exceeds 0.4\,RTT; in 11 of them it is about one RTT, and the largest is 1.9\,RTT. Two stack mechanisms explain these errors.

\emph{Window validation.} The idealised model enlarges the window of a direction by the segments of every flight. In this model, the one-segment ClientHello enlarges the client window to 11, enough for the 11-segment client flight of this example. The observed Linux stack does not enlarge the window after this short flight, and the client flight pays one additional RTT. This is the half of the window rule that the 226 configurations test; the other half, that a flight which fills the window enlarges it by less than its segment count, never changes a later flight in them and rests on the plain-TCP measurement below. For ML-DSA-65 at depth~2, an MTU of 1\,500\,bytes and a 30\ms RTT, the testbed measures 148.9\ms. The idealised model predicts 121.8\ms; the model with window validation predicts 150.7\ms.

\emph{Segmentation.} The kernel data-segment counters show more segments per flight than $\lceil\text{bytes}/\mathrm{MSS}\rceil$. A system-call trace (\texttt{strace}) of OpenSSL shows the cause. Handshake messages leave through a 4\,096-byte buffer; for ML-DSA-65 at depth~1, the server writes 4\,096, 4\,096 and 2\,688\,bytes. Sosnowski et~al.\ report the same buffer in a patched OpenSSL~\cite{sosnowski2023}. The server segment counts that Dubetskyy and Cano~\cite{dubetskyy2026} captured for five combinations of key exchange and certificate at three MTUs (4 to 13 segments per connection) follow from this write pattern in all 15 cases and from $\lceil\text{bytes}/\mathrm{MSS}\rceil$ in 11; the header size does not matter, but the two session tickets and the closing alert must leave as three segments of their own (none of 5 combinations match with 0, 1, 2 or 4 segments). We made this calculation after their numbers were published (\texttt{analyze\_dubetskyy\_segments.py}, \texttt{results/dubetskyy\_segments\_20261116.txt}). The request is a separate write, so each write boundary can leave a partial segment (\cref{sec:transport}). Neither mechanism suffices alone (\cref{S-tab:ablation}): the MAE is 3.34\ms with window validation only, 3.15\ms with the write boundaries only and 1.65\ms with both, against 4.70\ms for the idealised model. The idealised model predicts the exact segment count in 78 of 300 flights (the two directions of each of 150 configurations with kernel counters; the server-to-client count excludes the 24-byte close\_notify alert that the server sends right after the response, which the client's counter has usually already registered). The model with this write pattern predicts 237 of them. In the remaining flights, the measured flight almost always contains one additional segment, mostly with the larger initial window, and the latency of these runs does not change. The effect matters when a flight lies just below the window. ML-DSA-65 at depth~1 and an MTU of 1\,280\,bytes has nine segments in each of its two long flights in the idealised model and eleven to twelve in the measurement. It pays two RTTs more than predicted: 147.3\ms measured, 91.9\ms in the idealised model and 150.4\ms with the write pattern.

\emph{Simpler baselines.} Two simpler rules could stand in for the stack model, and we compare them with it on the same 226 configurations (\cref{tab:baselines}); both were defined after the data were collected and are comparisons, not pre-registered predictions. The size-versus-window rule follows the size analyses of the literature~\cite{kampanakis2022}: a flight pays one extra round trip if it has more segments than the initial window, and none otherwise. The stateless rule is textbook slow start per flight: every flight starts from the initial window and no window is carried between flights. Both leave 12 to 26 configurations more than 0.4 RTT in error, against 4 for the stack model, so the errors come from the two stack behaviours and not from arithmetic that any slow-start rule would supply. On the x86 repeat, the i9 and the Arm run on Linux~7.0 (\cref{sec:repl}) the ordering is the same (stack 0.9--2.4\ms, stateless 2.9--4.4, size-versus-window 3.8--4.8, idealised 4.4--5.9).

\emph{Window rule measured without TLS.} A separate plain-TCP experiment on the same kernel (a fresh connection, a burst of $n$ full segments, a reply that acknowledges all of it, then the window read from \texttt{TCP\_INFO}) measured the window after an $n$-segment flight as $\max(w_0,\min(w_0+n,2n))$ in 12 of 13 tested configurations (data in the public repository), for the tested RTTs, rates, initial windows ($w_0$ in the formula), delayed acknowledgements, CUBIC, timestamps and offload. The window rule of the model, derived from the replay of the ACK clock, differs from this closed form by one segment at odd $n>w_0$; none of the 238 full-handshake configurations measured (the 226 above and 12 with larger requests) is affected. At an MTU of 9\,000\,bytes, the measured window deviates from the closed form for $n\ge 8$. The advertised receive window is then about 63\,000\,bytes, seven segments, and the sender is limited by it: with the receive buffer raised from 128\,KiB to 1\,MiB, 16 of 20 flight sizes follow the closed form (7 of 20 by default). The other four stay one to two segments below it.

\subsection{Prediction accuracy and residual errors}
With the two mechanisms, the MAE is 1.65\ms, the bias is $+0.10$\ms, the maximum error is 5.4\ms. The model lies within 1\ms of the measurement in 110 of 226 configurations. The maximum error is 3.9, 5.4 and 3.8\ms at RTTs of 5, 30 and 100\ms. The maximum error does not grow with the RTT, which fits residual processing or timing offsets; an omitted RTT step would scale with the RTT. The measurement is more precise than the model. The model lies inside the 95\% bootstrap interval of the measured median in 46 of 226 configurations, which indicates a systematic residual beyond measurement uncertainty. Processing overlap is a candidate cause that we did not isolate. The RMSE is 2.16\ms. The MAE equals 1.2\% of the mean excess latency, and its 95\% bootstrap interval is 1.48--1.83\ms (\cref{S-tab:residuals,S-fig:residuals}).

In the card-authorisation example the model puts the two calls at 91.0 and 153.7\ms, and the testbed measured 94.1 and 157.1\ms. The model is thus about 3\ms low on both, but it reproduces the 63\ms between them to within a millisecond, which is the quantity that the example is about.

\emph{What the error measures.} The two questions the model answers should be read separately. For the round-trip steps, the idealised model is wrong by more than half an RTT and more than 5\ms in 19 of the 226 configurations (errors of 15 to 97\ms), the stack model in none; three of its errors exceed 5\ms and the largest is 5.4\ms. The four configurations of the stack model above 0.4\,RTT all lie at a 5\ms RTT, where that bound is 2\ms. The absolute latency is a different matter. Here the accuracy is of the order of one to two milliseconds with a systematic component, and the MAE ranges from 0.64 to 2.14\ms across suites (\cref{tab:testbed,S-tab:residuals}). The model locates the round-trip steps reliably on the tested stack; it does not predict every latency to the millisecond, and the overall MAE hides that spread.

\emph{Scope at an MTU of 9\,000 bytes.} The stack mechanisms do not improve the prediction there: the MAE of the 48 configurations is 2.17\ms for the stack model and 2.11\ms for the idealised one, against 1.87 and 8.22\ms at 1\,280 and 1.33 and 4.03\ms at 1\,500 bytes. The model has no receive-window limit, which limits plain TCP at this MTU (\cref{sec:mech}); we did not model it, and the TLS errors also coincide in part with the initial window of 32 segments. The validation therefore supports the model at MTUs of 1\,280 and 1\,500 bytes (178 configurations, MAE 1.51\ms) and does not at 9\,000 bytes.

\emph{Full handshakes at an MTU of 9\,000 bytes.} A pre-registered experiment tested the stack model there: 24 cells (three suites, responses of 9 to 16 segments, 100\ms RTT, 100\Mbits) on the Arm machine and on the i9. Its accuracy criterion (at least 80\% of the cells within 2\ms with a raised receive buffer) failed: 2 of 24 cells on the Arm machine and 15 of 24 on the i9. Two things cause the misses. One response size, 88\,700 bytes, is a whole round trip above the model in all three suites on both machines ($+95$ to $+98$\ms), with both buffers; the receive window does not explain it, because the model counts ten segments, below the ten-segment window, whereas the wire carries twelve data segments. The other 21 cells show an almost constant offset of the machine and session ($+3.4$\ms on the Arm machine, $+1.4$\ms on the i9; \cref{sec:repl}). A check made after the data were seen, and therefore not pre-registered, traced the first miss in part to the model: OpenSSL writes one record of at most 16\,384 bytes at a time, whereas the model writes a message at once. With the option of one write per record, which is off in every other result of this paper, no cell is more than 50\ms from the model. A later test with criteria written before the run (20 full-handshake cells at an MTU of 1\,500 bytes) confirmed the option in one band of response sizes (ML-DSA-65, depth~1) and not in another (depth~2), and its criteria on the flip cells and on the accuracy were not met. The explanation is therefore partial, and the model keeps a flight-size error of about one round trip in narrow bands of the response size for ML-DSA-65 chains. The other numbers of both checks are in Supplementary Appendix~\ref{S-app:records}.

\emph{Location of the error.} No parameter of the stack model was fitted to these measurements. We found the two behaviours on the core grid of 82 configurations at 100\Mbits and an initial window of 10 segments, so this grid is not independent of the model. The 144 configurations that vary the bit rate, the MTU and the initial window come from a later campaign of the same day on the same machine (Supplementary Appendix~\ref{S-app:more}). The MAE is 0.96\ms on the core grid and 2.04\ms off the grid (1.98\ms for the 96 off-grid configurations with an MTU of 1\,280 or 1\,500 bytes, 2.17\ms for the 48 at 9\,000 bytes, so the jumbo frames do not account for the difference). The absolute MAE is 0.92--1.84\ms across the three RTTs. The relative error is largest at a 5\ms RTT (4.9\% of the excess latency) and smallest at 100\ms (0.2\%). The error depends on the setting (\cref{S-tab:residuals}): the MAE is 2.35\ms at an initial window of 32 segments against 1.32\ms at 10. It does not depend on the chain depth (1.58--1.72\ms). Of the 226 configurations, 178 use a 30\ms RTT. The bias is $-1.5$\ms for ECDSA-P256 with X25519, $-0.6$\ms for the hybrid suite, $+0.9$\ms for RSA-2048 and $+0.7$ to $+1.0$\ms for the ML-DSA suites. The model overstates the ML-DSA increment over the ECDSA baseline by 2.2--2.5\ms. The largest error (5.4\ms; ML-DSA-65, depth~2, 30\ms RTT, MTU of 9\,000\,bytes, initial window of 32 segments) is a fraction of one RTT, and it falls in the MTU range that the validation excludes (see the scope paragraph above).

\emph{Replication on other machines.}\label{sec:repl} We repeated the 226-configuration validation and the plain-TCP window test on a second x86 machine (i9-10900K, Linux~7.0) and on an Arm machine (the GX10, Linux~6.14 twice, eight hours apart, and Linux~7.0), and the window test also on four kernels of the first machine (6.8, 6.14, 6.17, 7.0); the criteria were written before the runs. The closed form for the window holds at every flight size in the base, MTU~1\,280 and initial-window-20 configurations on all eight machine--kernel points, and at 9\,000 bytes only 35--40\% of the flight sizes follow it with the default buffer on all of them. The model error (\cref{tab:repl}) is of the same size on every machine, the idealised model is 2.5 to 4.7 times worse, and the stack model meets the 2\ms criterion at MTU $\le 1\,500$ in four of the six runs (it misses it, at 2.4--2.5\ms, in the two later Arm runs). At 9\,000 bytes the Arm machine on Linux~7.0 is within 0.5\ms of the original error (2.01 against 2.17\ms); the x86 repeat and the i9 are lower (1.63 and 1.07\ms).

\emph{What does not replicate.} The measured excess latency itself does not agree to within a millisecond: the pre-registered criterion (1\ms in 90\% of the configurations) fails for every pair of runs, including two runs of the same Arm machine (median difference 1.2\ms, with an identical unshaped baseline, so the change lies in the shaped part). The kernel is not the cause (four kernels of one machine differ by 0.04--0.14\ms in the median); the difference belongs to the machine and the session: against the first machine, the i9 differs at 10\Mbits only (+1.3\ms), and the Arm machine by +1.4 to +2.5\ms in three runs on two kernels. A probe of the link without TCP found that the smallest round trip of a single packet exceeds the nominal one by 1.1--1.3\ms on the Arm machine; holding the CPU idle-state latency at zero (\texttt{/dev/cpu\_dma\_latency}) in a probe without TCP removed that excess on the Arm machine (31.0 and 31.3\ms without the hold, 30.0\ms with it, in an A-B-A sequence of 50 round trips each), so the wake-up latency of idle cores is a candidate cause; on the X250 the same hold shortened the probe round trip by 0.5\ms and lengthened the transactions by 1.7\ms, and we did not repeat the transaction test on the Arm machine. The model is therefore trusted for structure, the whole round trips that the windows add, and not for offsets below about 2.5\ms; an absolute error of one to two milliseconds is within that offset. The stack mechanism of \cref{sec:mech} is reproduced, and sub-millisecond accuracy is not claimed.

\section{Robustness and the limits of the model}
\label{sec:robust}

Does the validated model survive other network and TCP settings, and a measured middlebox? This section tests that, and says where the evidence stops. We put the failures before the design questions of \cref{sec:results} on purpose. A reader should know how far to trust the instrument before listening to what it says.

\subsection{Additional network and TCP settings}
The 144 off-grid configurations vary the bit rate (10 and 1\,000\Mbits), the MTU (up to 9\,000\,bytes) and the initial window (32 segments). Their MAE is 1.98\ms at MTUs of 1\,280 and 1\,500 bytes (96 configurations) and 2.17\ms at 9\,000 bytes (48). On this set, the idealised model has an MAE of 4.32\ms, and 11 configurations exceed 0.4\,RTT. The stack model has an MAE of 2.04\ms and a maximum error of 5.4\ms, and no configuration exceeds 0.4\,RTT. The sign of its bias follows the bit rate: $+1.1$\ms at 10\Mbits and $-1.2$\ms at 1\,000\Mbits. We did not trace this dependence to a single cause; the accounting of the rate limiter is one candidate.

\Cref{S-tab:variants} reports other TCP settings. With CUBIC~\cite{rfc9438} in place of Reno (216 configurations) the MAE is 1.19\ms (idealised: 5.43\ms), and delayed acknowledgements, TCP timestamps and both (72 configurations) give 1.26, 1.32 and 1.23\ms (idealised: about 11\ms); their excess latency differs from the Reno quick-acknowledgement run by a median of at most 0.15\ms. All three settings together (CUBIC, delayed acknowledgements and timestamps; 24 configurations of the core grid, run later) give an MAE of 2.5\ms against 1.25\ms for the same machine with quick acknowledgements, and no configuration above 0.4\,RTT. The round-trip steps are still located; the measured excess is 2.2\ms higher (median) than in the quick-acknowledgement run, an offset that the model does not carry and that is similar at 5, 30 and 100\ms, as expected of an offset and not of an omitted RTT step. Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time (BBR) congestion control lies outside the model: in an exploratory run of 8 configurations that cross a window boundary, the model under-predicted it by an MAE of 3.51\ms (maximum 21.8\ms, at a 100\ms RTT); this set calibrates nothing and supports no ranking of controllers.

\subsection{Measured TCP-proxy effect}
The term $T_\mathrm{pen}$ of \cref{eq:latency} applies only when a middlebox delays long flights. On the direct path, the least-squares fit gives $\pi=0.00$\ms at $\theta=1$ segment, and its RMSE (2.16\ms) equals the RMSE at $\pi=0$, so the threshold-delay term does not improve the fit. We then inserted HAProxy~2.8 as a TCP proxy between the two namespaces, once forwarding bytes on arrival and once waiting for the complete ClientHello before routing, in 16 configurations (ECDSA-P256, ML-DSA-44, -65 and -87 at chain depths 1--4, 30\ms RTT, 100\Mbits, MTU of 1\,500\,bytes, initial window of 10 segments, flights of 2--27 segments). The proxy adds a mean of 0.18\ms (range $-0.22$ to 0.31\ms) in the first mode and 0.28\ms (range $-0.50$ to 0.55\ms) in the second. No large size-dependent delay appears in this sample, which covers one proxy and one version; the swept values of $\pi$ up to 24\ms represent untested devices such as deep packet inspection, TLS-terminating proxies or hardware firewalls. The measurements support $\pi=0$, which the single values below use.

\subsection{Where the model stops: evidence boundaries and limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\emph{Stack and scope.} The main empirical comparison uses one Linux/OpenSSL stack on a single x86 host with isolated network namespaces (repeated on a second x86 host and an Arm host, \cref{sec:repl}), no configured packet loss and no cross traffic. The testbed validates excess latency for request-response probes, mainly with ISO~8583-sized messages and one SWIFT-sized comparison; it does not validate ISO~20022 or FAPI workflows against operational services. The runs with other TCP settings do not establish transferability to other operating systems, TLS libraries or kernels, and BBR lies outside the model. The link between the stack and financial deployments is an assumption of scope: gateways and API services that terminate TLS over TCP are exposed to the same two mechanisms, but we did not measure whether Linux/OpenSSL represents the Java, appliance or mainframe stacks of financial networks. On another stack the round trips may fall at other flights; Supplementary Appendices~\ref{S-app:assumptions} and~\ref{S-app:writes} give the stack assumptions with their evidence and the effect of the write pattern.

\emph{Further pre-registered tests.} Two tests, with IPv4 headers (which move some flights across the window) and with segmentation offload, are reported in Supplementary Appendix~\ref{S-app:records}. The IPv4 test confirmed the predicted round trip in the four ECDSA-P256 cells on three machines and missed it in four ML-DSA-65 cells on all three, a miss that the one-record-at-a-time option of the model explains after the fact. Offload changed the median excess by 0.2 to 0.5\ms on two machines, and a capture on the Arm machine confirmed that the veth pair carries merged segments.

\emph{Session tickets.} The testbed and the model use full handshakes without session tickets, whereas production servers issue them. In a follow-up experiment a full transaction that also issued one ticket was half a round trip slower in 6 of 40 configurations (at 30 and 100\ms; \cref{S-tab:ticket}) and equal in the others, and a model with the ticket as an extra server write did not reproduce the effect (\cref{S-app:extra}). The latencies reported here may therefore be up to half a round trip too low where tickets are issued, the model has no mode for it, and the results apply to full handshakes without tickets; the mechanism for resumed transactions is studied in follow-up work~\cite{dinh2026artifact}.

\emph{Clock and residual.} With the CPU clock fixed (\cref{sec:testbed}), frequency changes are unlikely to explain the residual of the validation sets. Subtraction of unshaped medians reduces shared local processing costs, but it cannot guarantee the cancellation of scheduling or network--computation overlap. The systematic residual of \cref{sec:testbed} stands: a low MAE is encouraging, but it does not mean agreement within the measurement uncertainty.

\emph{Inputs.} We measured the byte and CPU inputs for the supported components; Falcon, SLH-DSA and composite configurations did not run as full TLS transactions. Protocol message sizes and the default XMLDSig certificate embedding are scenario choices. The model writes each application message at once; all validated messages are shorter than the 16\,KiB record that OpenSSL writes at a time (Supplementary Appendix~\ref{S-app:records}). The main sweep gives both mTLS endpoints the same chain depth (\cref{S-tab:asym} varies them separately), and chains in which a certificate of one algorithm is signed by a CA of another algorithm, a staged-migration pattern that Paul et al.\ measured on Internet paths~\cite{paul2022}, are modelled only in one scenario without measurement here (\cref{S-tab:mixed}). The average increment $\bar{\Delta}_d$ is a simultaneous depth increment that adds a certificate at each endpoint; it is not the marginal delay of one certificate.

\emph{Server processing and library version.} A direct measurement of an event-driven OpenSSL~3.5.4 server (\cref{S-tab:srvcpu}) puts the CPU per full handshake at 1.2 to 1.3 times the primitive costs of the model for the post-quantum and RSA suites and at 1.9--2.8 times for the classical ECDSA suites, within the CPU scales of 1 to 16 swept here; the excess over the primitives is a per-connection cost of the server that the model does not include (\cref{S-app:extra}). We measured no HSM, middleware, queue, database or backend payment process, and Project Leap Phase~2 reports receiver-side verification of 209.9\ms for its integrated Dilithium application~\cite{bis_leap2025}, much larger than the primitive costs here, with a different processing scope and different algorithms. The finding that transport overhead $O$ exceeds the modelled primitive time therefore applies to the modelled operations only, not to all processing behind a payment gateway, and to the costs of OpenSSL~3.5.4. With the primitive costs of OpenSSL~4.1.0, which signs with ML-DSA 63\% (Arm) to 70\% (x86) faster and verifies 54\% faster, the transport-bound share of ML-DSA-65 rises from 75.6\% to 83--84\% and the depth effect stays at 59.7\ms (60.0\ms with 3.5.4): the conclusion that transport dominates holds for both library versions, but the percentages are those of 3.5.4 (\cref{S-app:extra}). The order of the signature schemes on untested stacks is open.

\emph{Intermediaries and extrapolations.} HAProxy is the only measured intermediary, and its sub-millisecond added delay does not validate nonzero threshold delays for deep packet inspection, TLS termination or hardware firewalls. Random packet loss of 0 to 2\% was tested on one machine (Supplementary Appendix~\ref{S-app:loss}): the median and the mean follow the model, the tail (95th and 99th percentiles) does not. Bursty loss, receive-window limits (\cref{sec:mech}), wide-area jitter, competing traffic, queueing and operational tail latency are not validated in this article. Bit rates above 1\,000\Mbits and RTTs outside 5, 30 and 100\ms are model extrapolations. The 90\% uncertainty intervals are conditional on assumed input distributions (\cref{sec:rq5}); they are not confidence intervals for a population of financial deployments.

Which bytes the migration adds is already answered in \cref{sec:method} (\cref{S-fig:sizes}). Four further questions follow: depth, cost split, compression, and how far the answers can be trusted.

\subsection{How much does a longer chain cost?}
Return first to the card-authorisation example, because its depth series, measured on the testbed, is the plainest picture of what the rest of this subsection quantifies. Suppose a bank first migrates only the signatures of a minimal chain, in which each side sends just its own certificate, signed directly by the root. With ML-DSA-65 the call takes 97.5\ms against 93.0\ms with ECDSA-P256, a difference that no one would chase: both flights have eight or nine segments and fit in the window of ten. Then the PKI team adds an intermediate authority, as such teams do to separate duties or to keep the root far from daily use. The call now takes 153.8\ms. One more certificate on each side has pushed the two flights to 13 and 12 segments, and each pays an extra round trip. A third and a fourth level add only 3.3 and 1.8\ms, because the flights are already past the window and the waiting is already paid; the ECDSA-P256 call moves from 93.0 to 94.6\ms over the same four depths. The cost of a certificate, in other words, is not a constant: the second one costs 56\ms and the fourth costs two. This is why \emph{where} a flight lies relative to the window matters more than how large it is. \Cref{fig:cliff} plots the series.

Under each depth, the model's segments of the ML-DSA-65 server flight and client flight; the flights above the window of 10 segments are in red. The jump of 56\ms between depths~1 and~2 is where both flights first exceed the window.}
\label{fig:cliff}

\Cref{fig:depth} shows SWIFT~MX latency against chain depth for three MTUs at 100\Mbits, a 30\ms RTT and $\pi=0$, on a logarithmic scale. The latency grows in whole RTTs. The classical baseline gains $+29.6$\ms from depth 1 to 4, about one RTT, because from depth~2 its client flight, a handshake flight followed by a separately written request of about 11\,KiB, exceeds the initial window of ten segments. ML-DSA-65 gains $+60.0$\ms, two RTTs, of which slow-start waiting contributes 56.5\ms, serialisation 2.9\ms and computation 0.6\ms. The post-quantum-specific cost of three simultaneous depth increments is the difference, 30.5\ms (computed from unrounded values), or about 10.2\ms per simultaneous chain-depth increment.

We define the average increment as $\bar{\Delta}_d=(T_{d=4}-T_{d=1})/3$; it averages three increments at the two peers and is not the marginal cost of one certificate, and the average hides the shape that the card-authorisation series shows. For ML-DSA-65 at an MTU of 1\,500\,bytes, $\bar{\Delta}_d=20.0$\ms in absolute terms (3.3\ms at a 5\ms RTT and 66.7\ms at a 100\ms RTT).

Three parameters move the depth effect. Jumbo frames (MTU of 9\,000\,bytes) reduce $\bar{\Delta}_d$ to 1.1\ms, a model output outside the validated range (\cref{sec:repl}), and a delay of $\pi=24$\ms raises the jumbo-frame value to 9.1\ms (\cref{S-tab:depth,S-tab:interaction,S-tab:threshold}). An initial window of 32 segments halves the depth effect to $+29.9$\ms.

The two chains contribute separately (\cref{S-tab:asym}): growing only the server chain from depth 1 to 4 adds 30.7\ms and only the client chain 29.3\ms, which sum to the 60.0\ms; each chain pays one RTT, at different depths, since each direction has its own window. The size of the chain comes mainly from the CA keys and signatures: in a model-only scenario with a post-quantum leaf key under classical CAs the depth effect almost disappears ($+0.7$\ms), and with a classical leaf under post-quantum CAs it is $+32.4$\ms (\cref{S-tab:mixed}). Only the first case authenticates the whole chain with post-quantum signatures; the others are what a staged migration might look like and are not secure against a quantum adversary where the classical algorithm remains.

The location of each flight depends on the stack (\cref{sec:testbed}). In the stack model the hybrid suite is not faster than the baseline (122 vs.\ 121\ms; the testbed measures 121.2 vs.\ 121.0\ms on SWIFT-sized transactions), the composite suite follows ML-DSA-65 ($+60.4$ vs.\ $+60.0$\ms), and RSA-2048 behaves like the baseline from depth~2 ($+0.6$\ms), because its largest flight already has 12 segments at depth 1. ML-DSA-87 shows a smaller depth effect than ML-DSA-65 ($+32.3$ vs.\ $+60.0$\ms) since its flights already cross an additional window boundary at depth~1. The idealised model gets these cases wrong: it makes the hybrid suite faster than the baseline at an MTU of 1\,280\,bytes (92 vs.\ 121\ms), gives the composite suite $+32.8$\ms against $+59.9$\ms for ML-DSA-65, and predicts a flat classical baseline ($+0.6$\ms). The ML-DSA-65 depth effect of about 60\ms does not depend on these details, nor on the write pattern of the TLS library (Supplementary Appendix~\ref{S-app:writes}), but the classical baseline's does: with larger certificates (leaf 1\,400, CA 800 bytes) its chain already crosses the window at depth 1 and its depth effect falls from 29.6 to 0.7\ms, so the post-quantum-specific difference of 30.5\ms is conditional on the certificate metadata (\cref{sec:sens,S-tab:sens}).

\subsection{Is the network or the cryptography the larger cost?}
Which dominates the added latency: the size-dependent transport overhead $O$ or the modelled cryptographic primitives $T_\cpu$? $T_\cpu$ is the sum of the primitive operations on the serial critical path of the model. It leaves out HSMs, middleware, queues, databases and backend processing, so a share above one half does not mean that the network takes more time than the gateway as a whole. The metric is $\phi$ of \cref{eq:share}, a fraction of equally weighted grid rows. With $\pi=0$, $O>T_\cpu$ holds in 44.2\% of the baseline rows and in 75.6\% of the ML-DSA-65 rows. \Cref{tab:master} gives the shares for the measured suites and \cref{S-tab:master_full} for all thirteen. Larger values of $\pi$ are explored only in the Supplementary Material.

At CPU scale 4, which corresponds to a measured x86 core, the shares are 25\% for the baseline, 38\% for Falcon-512 and 61\% for ML-DSA-65: the ML-DSA-65 share stays above one half and the baseline below it. The serial model charges all primitive steps on its critical path; an assumed overlap that removes half or three-quarters of this charge gives 67\% and 76\% for ML-DSA-65. This is an arithmetic sensitivity check, not a measurement of overlap.

\Cref{S-fig:ratio} shows the ratio of transport overhead to cryptographic time for the reference SWIFT~MX profile with $\pi=0$. Above 100\Mbits the ratio changes little with the bit rate, since the remaining ACK waiting depends on flight size, window state and RTT. SLH-DSA-128s is the main computation-bound exception. Its four online signatures contribute most of the 685\ms primitive budget per transaction, whereas SLH-DSA-128f produces larger authentication traffic.


\subsection{Does certificate compression help?}
Keys and signatures are pseudorandom and do not compress. Compression removes 31\% of an ECDSA-P256 chain and only 5\% of an ML-DSA-65 chain, and it saves at least 1\ms in just 5--21\% of the configurations: the gain depends on whether compression moves a flight below a window boundary. For ML-DSA-65, the maximum saving on this grid is about one RTT. Other suites exceed this bound (\cref{S-tab:rq4}). Decompression can cost more than compression saves. At an assumed client decompression rate of 3.3\,MB/s, compression is net-harmful in 95\% of the ML-DSA-65 configurations and in 0\% of the ECDSA-P256 configurations. The median break-even decompression rate for ML-DSA-65 is about 13\,MB/s (\cref{S-tab:rq4,S-fig:cc}). Zlib at level 9 reduces real ML-DSA and SLH-DSA chains to 0.88--0.98 of their size, within 0.05 of the modelled ratio (\cref{S-tab:comp_real}).

\subsection{How far can the answers be trusted?}
\label{sec:sens}\label{sec:rq5}
\emph{Input variants.} We varied the certificate metadata (600--1\,400\,bytes per leaf), the XMLDSig certificate embedding and the window of reused connections (\cref{S-tab:sens}). Omission of the signer certificate from XMLDSig reduces the ML-DSA-65 SWIFT~MX transaction from 83.4 to 67.2\,KiB. A reused connection that keeps a large window ($c=64$) reduces the warm ML-DSA-65 ISO~20022 exchange from 92 to 34\ms (baseline: 31\ms). The reference comparisons keep the order Falcon-512 $<$ ML-DSA-65 $<$ SLH-DSA-128f in the evaluated input variants; this order compares selected parameter sets and is not universal over security categories or stacks.

\emph{Global sensitivity.} A variance-based analysis over the full factorial designs computes first- and second-order indices (\cref{S-tab:sobol}). The RTT explains 0.59 of the variance of log latency, and the suite explains 0.28. For the post-quantum factor of the lattice schemes, the MTU explains 0.18, the RTT 0.20 and the suite 0.16.

\emph{Propagated uncertainty.} The deterministic model propagates selected input uncertainty; structural model error and unmeasured deployment conditions remain outside this analysis. We drew 4\,000 samples of the certificate metadata (600--1\,400\,bytes per leaf), the CPU scale (log-uniform on $[1,16]$, an assumed range of primitive-processing slowdowns) and the initial window (10, 32 or 64 segments with probabilities 0.6, 0.3 and 0.1), with $\pi=0$ because the testbed found no size-dependent delay (\cref{sec:testbed}; the draws that also vary $\pi$ and $\theta$ are in \cref{S-tab:uncertainty}). For each sample we recomputed a SWIFT~MX transaction at depth~2, 100\Mbits and a 30\ms RTT. The priors are assumptions, not fitted distributions, so the intervals are conditional on them and are not confidence intervals for the performance of any bank; they exclude structural uncertainty from other operating systems, TLS libraries and middleboxes, jitter, packet loss and institution-specific traffic. The order Falcon-512 $<$ ML-DSA-65 $<$ SLH-DSA-128f holds in 100\% of the draws. The overhead $O$ exceeds the modelled primitive time in 71\% of the draws (80\% for CPU scales below 4 and 59\% for scales of 12--16). The ML-DSA-65 latency factor relative to the baseline has a median of 1.33 (90\% interval 1.09--1.54) and the Falcon-512 factor 1.02 (1.01--1.03). The average cost of about 20\ms per simultaneous ML-DSA-65 depth increment holds for the default window of 10 segments (median 20.7\ms) and falls to 10.7 and 1.8\ms at initial windows of 32 and 64 segments.

Earlier work had already pointed to authentication size and the initial window as the relevant factors. What the present measurements add is the link from those factors to the individual flights that end up waiting for ACKs, which is where an operator can act. The accuracy reported in \cref{sec:testbed} sets how the predictions should be read. A step of one whole RTT, or two, is a finding: the stack model located such steps reliably where the idealised model missed them. A difference of a few milliseconds between two designs is not, because it lies within the systematic residual.

The application layer deserves more attention than it usually gets. In the reference ML-DSA-65 SWIFT~MX transaction the mTLS chains carry the largest share of the bytes and the signed application objects with embedded certificates the next (\cref{tab:decisions}), and two cheap changes, referring to the signer certificate and keeping the window of a reused connection, remove a large part of the cost. Where should the operator of the card-authorisation example begin? With the two flights of 13 and 12 segments at depth~2, because everything else already fits in the window. More generally, deployment decisions should start from the actual certificate path, the application envelope and the life of the connection. Operators can remove unnecessary transmitted certificates and redundant embedding where trust and protocol requirements allow. On controlled paths, a larger initial window can remove RTT steps (validated here at 32 segments); a larger MTU may too, but the model is not validated at 9\,000 bytes. Both are subject to checks of path support, congestion effects and other flows. There is no universally best intervention here, and no maximum acceptable chain depth. The answer depends on the path.

Signature selection depends on security category and implementation constraints. Falcon-512 has smaller modelled authentication traffic than ML-DSA-65, but the two do not form an equal-security comparison. SLH-DSA-128s is computation-bound under the measured primitive implementation, and SLH-DSA-128f produces substantially more traffic. The SLH-DSA figures that involve messages above 16\,KiB depend on a write pattern that has been validated only for resumed transactions (Supplementary Appendix~\ref{S-app:records}), so we draw no conclusion from them about the order of the depth effects of the schemes; the order of their total latencies is unchanged. These results quantify performance costs for the evaluated parameters, not deployment readiness. \Cref{S-tab:status} records the specifications and measured components.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Post-quantum authentication increases transport latency when certificate-chain and application-signature bytes make an authentication flight cross the available TCP congestion window. The cost is threshold-driven rather than proportional to message size: once a flight exceeds the window it waits for acknowledgements and may add one or more round trips. By combining the observed OpenSSL handshake-write pattern with Linux slow-start window validation, the model connects the sizes of authentication objects to individual TLS flights and predicts where window crossings occur on the tested configurations.

Across 226 testbed configurations the model achieves a mean absolute error of 1.65\ms for configuration-level excess latency, against 4.70\ms for an idealised model, and 2.04\ms on 144 later configurations that were not used to identify the mechanisms. Repeated tests on a second x86 machine and an Arm machine reproduce the round-trip-scale steps, and measurements on four Linux kernels support the observed window rule. These results strengthen the evidence that the two stack mechanisms explain the additional round trips, but not sub-millisecond accuracy: only 46 of the 226 predictions fall within the measured 95\% bootstrap interval, and offsets of the machine and session reach 2.5\ms. The model should therefore be read as a predictor of round-trip-scale latency changes, not as a precise estimator of absolute latency across machines.

The validation also defines its limits. The measured transactions use full TLS~1.3 handshakes without session tickets over an emulated IPv6 link without configured packet loss or competing traffic (random loss of up to 2\% is tested in Supplementary Appendix~\ref{S-app:loss}). Six suites have full TLS measurements, mainly with ISO~8583-sized requests and one SWIFT-sized exchange; the other suites and protocol profiles are model applications, not measurements of operational financial workflows. The pre-registered full-handshake tests at an MTU of 9\,000 bytes failed their accuracy criterion (a whole round trip at one response size, a constant offset elsewhere); writing messages one TLS record at a time explains only part of that error, and in a pre-registered IPv4 test the predicted round trip appeared in four of eight cells on every machine, short of the criterion. Jumbo-MTU predictions and application messages above 16\,KiB therefore remain outside the validated domain. Supplementary Appendix~\ref{S-app:prereg} lists all 19 pre-registered criteria: 9 met, 9 not met, 1 met in part.

Within the evaluated scenarios the model shows why certificate-chain depth and application signatures should be considered together. In the reference SWIFT~MX scenario, raising both ML-DSA-65 chain depths from 1 to 4 adds 60.0\ms at a 30\ms RTT, of which 56.5\ms is slow-start waiting; in the card-authorisation example, the 63\ms between the classical and the ML-DSA-65 call are two extra round trips (three become five) although the payment message is unchanged. With no middlebox delay, and with primitive costs measured on the Cortex-X925 with OpenSSL~3.5.4, transport overhead exceeds the modelled primitive time in 75.6\% of the equally weighted ML-DSA-65 cold-connection grid (61\% at CPU scale 4, the measured x86 core). These percentages describe the selected model grid; they estimate neither deployment prevalence nor the total processing cost of a financial gateway, and we did not measure any effect on the people who wait for the call.

The practical implication, argued in \cref{sec:discussion}, is to locate the authentication flight that crosses the congestion window before deciding which bytes to remove. In the model, omitting unnecessary certificates and embedded signer certificates where protocol and trust requirements allow, reusing established connections and enlarging the initial window on controlled paths remove some round-trip steps (only the window of 32 segments was validated on the testbed), whereas certificate compression helps little in most evaluated configurations; no figure per certificate carries over to another stack. Future work should test other TLS stacks, ticketed and resumed connections, wide-area paths with loss and competing traffic, and operational payment gateways, to establish how far these thresholds transfer beyond the tested Linux/OpenSSL environment.
