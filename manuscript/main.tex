\begin{abstract}
Post-quantum signatures are one to two orders of magnitude larger than their classical counterparts and enlarge the certificate chains and signed objects of mutual Transport Layer Security (mTLS) payment transactions. Their cost is paid mainly in round trips: a flight that exceeds the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) congestion window waits one more round-trip time (RTT), and message size alone does not tell which flight does. We propose a per-segment transport model that locates these thresholds from two observed behaviors of the tested stack, the OpenSSL write pattern and Linux slow-start window validation. On 226 kernel-emulated configurations, the model predicts excess latency with a mean absolute error (MAE) of 1.65\ms, against 4.70\ms and whole-round-trip errors for an idealized closed-form model. The 144 configurations measured later, not used to identify the mechanisms, give 2.04\ms, with a systematic residual of a few milliseconds. The validation covers ISO~8583-sized requests and one SWIFT-sized transaction; other profiles and suites are model applications. Applied to thirteen suites and six financial protocol profiles, the model shows that raising both ML-DSA-65 (Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm) chain depths from 1 to 4 in a SWIFT~MX transaction adds 60.0\ms at a 30\ms RTT, mostly slow-start waiting, and that size-dependent transport overhead exceeds the modeled cryptographic-primitive time in 75.6\% of the equally weighted ML-DSA-65 cold-connection grid without an assumed middlebox delay. Chain depth, certificate embedding and the initial window set the transport-layer overhead on the tested stack.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A payment gateway that uses mutual Transport Layer Security (mTLS) exchanges two certificate chains at connection establishment and may sign each payment request and response. Migration to post-quantum authentication expands these objects at the transport and application layers. An ML-DSA-65 signature occupies 3\,309\,bytes~\cite{fips204}; an Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA) P-256 signature occupies at most 72\,bytes in Distinguished Encoding Rules (DER) encoding~\cite{fips186}. A flight is a group of messages that one endpoint sends before waiting for the peer. When a flight exceeds the available Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) congestion window, the sender waits for acknowledgments (ACKs), and the delay grows with the round-trip time (RTT).

The migration follows from the vulnerability of Rivest–Shamir–Adleman (RSA) and elliptic-curve cryptography to a sufficiently large quantum computer~\cite{shor1994}. For confidentiality, the migration schedule depends on the lifetime of recorded information~\cite{mosca2018,baradziej2026}. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) standardized the Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism (ML-KEM), the Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (ML-DSA) and the Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (SLH-DSA) in 2024~\cite{fips203,fips204,fips205}. Studies in this journal assess the quantum risk to network protocols and infrastructure~\cite{baseri2024,baseri2026}, and central-bank pilots have demonstrated post-quantum tunnels and signed financial messages~\cite{bis_leap2023,bis_leap2025}. Selecting certificate chains and message-signing profiles requires predicting the latency overhead that each authenticated transaction adds at the transport layer.

The dominant approach estimates this overhead from object sizes and the initial congestion window. Three limitations remain. First, measurement studies report handshake slowdown for given certificate sizes and initial windows, and they do not predict which flight incurs an additional RTT on a specified implementation~\cite{sikeridis2020,paquin2020}. Evaluation frameworks for post-quantum TLS measure handshake throughput and bytes under controlled loss and delay~\cite{montenegro2026} but do not predict individual flights either. Second, size analyses compare flight bytes with the initial window expressed in bytes~\cite{kampanakis2022}. This comparison is equivalent to dividing the flight size by the maximum segment size (MSS) and rounding up. The tested OpenSSL library writes handshake data through a 4\,096-byte buffer, which also decides when the ServerHello leaves~\cite{sosnowski2023}, and the Linux kernel validates window growth before enlarging the window~\cite{linuxtcp}. To our knowledge, no earlier study uses these two behaviors to predict which flights cross the window. Each mechanism changes the segment count or the window available to the next flight. Finally, financial-system pilots measure tunnel setup and message signing but do not decompose the latency overhead of mTLS chains and application signatures~\cite{bis_leap2023,bis_leap2025}. Extensible Markup Language Signature (XMLDSig), JSON Web Signature (JWS) and Demonstrating Proof of Possession (DPoP) objects add bytes above TLS.

This paper develops a model that links authentication objects to library writes and TCP waiting; by transport-layer latency we mean the time that TCP and the way the TLS library writes into it add to a transaction. The scope is one Linux/OpenSSL stack on a wired link; the study introduces no cryptographic primitive and no security proof. The financial content of the model lies in the objects it carries: the certificate chains of the two mTLS peers and the application-layer signatures of the payment protocols. Linux/OpenSSL is an open, measurable choice of transport; we have no evidence that a particular bank gateway runs on it, and the write pattern of the library and the window rule of the kernel are replaceable inputs of the model. The study makes three contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item Method: we incorporate two observed stack mechanisms, the OpenSSL write pattern and Linux-style slow-start window validation, into a per-segment transport model.
\item Implementation: we apply the model to thirteen suites and six financial and application programming interface (API) profiles. Each profile includes the authentication objects of its scenario: the certificate chains of the two peers where the profile uses mTLS, and its application-layer signatures. The latency decomposition separates serialization, propagation, slow-start waiting, and cryptographic computation.
\item Evaluation: we compare the model with real OpenSSL transactions on 226 configurations. The mean absolute error (MAE) of configuration-level excess latency is 1.65\ms; the idealized closed-form model gives 4.70\ms. With the model, we quantify the effect of chain depth, certificate embedding, connection reuse and certificate compression, and report the sensitivity of each effect to the initial window and to the assumed processing cost.
\end{itemize}

The empirical evidence comes from synthetic bank-like certificates and request-response probes on a kernel-emulated wired link. The protocol sweep is a model application. It does not represent measured bank traffic or backend payment processing. Measurements of one TCP proxy define the tested middlebox case. Larger middlebox delays are hypothetical sensitivity inputs. \Cref{sec:related} reviews prior work, \cref{sec:method} defines the model, \cref{sec:testbed} presents the mechanisms and the validation, \cref{sec:results} reports the protocol-level predictions, \cref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations, and \cref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\paragraph{Authentication size and transport thresholds}
This direction measures how authentication size affects handshake latency. Early work established that the size of post-quantum certificates, not the computation, is the practical obstacle. Kampanakis et~al.\ analyzed how post-quantum X.509 certificates are fragmented in TLS and IKEv2 and tested the analysis experimentally~\cite{kampanakis2018}. Sikeridis et~al.\ measured TLS~1.3 with several signature candidates and certificate-chain compositions~\cite{sikeridis2020}. Their TLS and Secure Shell (SSH) study showed that a larger initial window reduces the post-quantum slowdown~\cite{sikeridis2020b}. Paquin et~al.\ evaluated latency and packet loss on a Linux-emulated network~\cite{paquin2020}. B{\"u}rstinghaus-Steinbach et~al.\ showed that hash-based signatures are costly on embedded servers~\cite{burstinghaus2020}. Raavi et~al.\ captured the TCP packetization of post-quantum TLS handshakes with a single certificate on local virtual machines~\cite{raavi2025}. Montenegro et~al.\ built a containerized OpenSSL framework and counted TLS handshakes per 10\,s and bytes per handshake for classical, hybrid and post-quantum key exchange and signatures, with mutual authentication, delays of 1, 10 and 100\ms and packet loss of 0.1--1\%; their certificate chain has two certificates of one algorithm, and the authors name other trust-chain configurations as future work~\cite{montenegro2026}. These studies establish the effect of authentication size, network conditions, and window settings. Sosnowski et~al.\ combined black- and white-box measurements of post-quantum TLS~1.3 under emulated loss, bandwidth and delay~\cite{sosnowski2023}. They observed that the internal buffer of OpenSSL decides when the ServerHello leaves, but did not use this to predict segments or round trips. These studies take byte counts, packet captures, and end-to-end times as the unit of analysis, with fixed or single-level chains. To our knowledge, no study combines the write pattern of the TLS library with kernel window growth to predict which flight crosses a window threshold on a specified implementation, and validates the prediction against real transactions.

\paragraph{Certificate reduction}
This direction reduces authentication traffic. Field measurements with enlarged certificate chains show that the initial window sets the handshake slowdown and that some clients or middleboxes fail at large handshake sizes~\cite{westerbaan2021}. Suppression of intermediate certificates reduces authentication bytes~\cite{kampanakis2022}. Key-based authentication replaces handshake signatures~\cite{schwabe2020}, and Merkle Tree Certificates replace per-certificate signatures with inclusion proofs~\cite{mtcdraft}. These approaches assume that fewer bytes yield lower latency. A byte reduction removes an RTT only when it moves a flight below a transport threshold. The locations of these thresholds for certificate-based TLS and application objects remain open.

\paragraph{Financial-system deployments}
This direction tests post-quantum cryptography in operational financial settings. Project Leap Phase~1 measured post-quantum IPsec tunnel establishment between central banks~\cite{bis_leap2023}. Project Leap Phase~2 evaluated post-quantum signing of ISO~20022 messages in a payment-system test environment with software keys and raw public keys. It reported receiver-side verification times of 209.9\ms for round-3 Dilithium category~3 and 28.1\ms for RSA~\cite{bis_leap2025}. The Banque de France trial deployed a post-quantum IPsec VPN with hardware security modules and Dilithium certificates, focused on interoperability and crypto-agility, and reported tunnel set-up times of 4 to 5\,s with FrodoKEM level~5 and the module; the report says that this result would have to be re-examined for a TLS connection~\cite{bdf2022}. The BdF-MAS trial demonstrated hybrid signed and encrypted e-mail between two central banks~\cite{bdfmas2024}. Surveys of quantum-safe network protocols and infrastructure identify migration and interoperability risk~\cite{baseri2024,baseri2026}. Supervisory and regulatory work frames the migration as a matter of planning, cryptographic inventory, and operational resilience~\cite{g7qtwg2026,andriani2024,dora2022,janciute2025}. These studies take integration, tunnel setup, and risk, or governance as the object of analysis. None of them measures certificate chains at more than one depth, and none of them decomposes the latency overhead of mTLS chains and application signatures in payment protocols. This decomposition is the gap that the present study addresses.

\subsection{Cryptographic suites and protocol profiles}
A suite pairs a key-establishment mechanism, a key exchange or a key-encapsulation mechanism (KEM), with one signature scheme. The signature scheme signs the certificate chain, the TLS \texttt{CertificateVerify} message and the application objects. \Cref{tab:algos} gives the public-key (PK), SubjectPublicKeyInfo (SPKI) and signature sizes in bytes and the median times on one Cortex-X925 core with OpenSSL~3.5.4 (liboqs~0.14.0 for Falcon). Its key-establishment rows give the TLS key-share sizes and the client and server computation. The classical baseline is ECDSA-P256 with the X25519 elliptic-curve Diffie--Hellman function. The classical suites add RSA-2048 and RSA-3072 with Elliptic Curve Diffie--Hellman Ephemeral (ECDHE) key exchange. ECDSA-P256 with the hybrid X25519MLKEM768 key share migrates key establishment only. The post-quantum suites pair ML-KEM with ML-DSA-44/65/87, with Falcon-512/1024~\cite{falcon2020} or with SLH-DSA-128s/128f. The composite suites use ML-DSA-44/65 + ECDSA-P256 certificates~\cite{compositedraft} with the hybrid key share of RFC~10024~\cite{hybriddraft}. The composite specification was an Internet-Draft in the RFC Editor queue at the time of writing. Sizes follow the cited specifications. The suites span different NIST security categories. Their performance ranking is not an equal-security comparison. Composite sizes and CPU costs are the sums of the constituent schemes; we did not measure interoperable composite objects. CPU costs are measured (\cref{sec:meas}). The sweeps scale them by 2 (the measured efficiency core), by 4 (close to the measured x86 core) and by 16 (an assumed slow-processing case). The factor 16 is not a measurement of a hardware security module (HSM).

The six profiles are ISO~8583 0200/0210 card messages protected by symmetric message authentication codes (MACs)~\cite{iso8583}; ISO~20022 pacs.008/pacs.002 payment messages~\cite{iso20022} and SWIFT~MX messages, each signed with XMLDSig~\cite{xmldsig} over mTLS; OAuth~2.0 with bearer tokens~\cite{rfc6749,rfc6750}; OpenID Connect (OIDC)~\cite{oidccore}; and Financial-grade API (FAPI)~2.0~\cite{fapi2} with signed request objects, DPoP~\cite{rfc9449} and mTLS~\cite{rfc8705}. SWIFT~MX denotes the ISO~20022-based message format of the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication (SWIFT). The profiles are representative scenario choices. They are not mandatory combinations of security mechanisms. The selected FAPI profile combines DPoP and mTLS. The XMLDSig profiles embed the signer certificate by default. Message sizes exclude algorithm-dependent bytes. The model adds encoded signatures, embedded public keys and signer certificates according to each profile. \Cref{sec:sens} evaluates a reference to the XML signer certificate in place of embedding.

\subsection{Authentication objects and transaction flow}
The size of a modeled certificate controls the bytes of each authentication flight. A certificate $i$ has length
\begin{equation}
L_i=m_i+b_\mathrm{SPKI}+b_\sigma+4,
\label{eq:cert}
\end{equation}
where $L_i$ is the encoded length in bytes, $m_i$ is the algorithm-independent metadata length, $b_\mathrm{SPKI}$ is the length of the SPKI field, $b_\sigma$ is the signature length and 4\,bytes is the outer DER framing. \Cref{eq:cert} separates fixed metadata from the algorithm-dependent key and signature fields. We set $m_i=900$\,bytes for a leaf and $m_i=520$\,bytes for a certification authority (CA) certificate (empirical choice; \cref{sec:sens} varies the two values). A transmitted chain of depth $d$ contains the leaf and $d-1$ intermediates and excludes the root~\cite{rfc5280}. Under mTLS, the same $d$ applies to the two endpoint chains in the main sweep; \cref{S-tab:asym} gives them separate depths. An increase of $d$ by one adds one transmitted intermediate at each endpoint.

A cold transaction opens a new TCP connection and performs a full TLS~1.3 handshake~\cite{rfc8446}. The ordered transfers are the ClientHello, the server authentication flight, the client authentication flight, a separately written first request and the remaining request--response exchanges. The model charges key establishment, handshake signatures, $d+1$ verification operations per authenticated chain (with \texttt{CertificateVerify}) and all application signatures. These CPU steps are serial on the modeled critical path. The model does not overlap certificate processing with the reception of the remaining flight bytes.

\subsection{Per-segment transport model and latency decomposition}
\label{sec:transport}
The reference path uses Internet Protocol version~6 (IPv6). The maximum transmission unit (MTU) and the MSS satisfy $\mathrm{MSS}=\mathrm{MTU}-60$\,bytes. Each direction maintains its own congestion window. The initial window is $c_0=10$ segments~\cite{rfc6928,rfc5681}. A warm transaction reuses TLS without a handshake. Its reference window restarts at $c_0$ and represents an idle connection. A sensitivity case retains a 64-segment window and represents a busy connection.

The ACK clock sets when each segment of a flight leaves the sender. Consider a flight of $N$ segments on a first-in, first-out (FIFO) link. The segment release time $r_k$, start time $s_k$, departure time $\ell_k$ and ACK return time $a_k$ follow
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
r_k&=\begin{cases}0,& k<c,\\a_{\lfloor(k-c)/2\rfloor},&k\ge c,\end{cases}\\
s_k&=\max(\ell_{k-1},r_k),\quad
\ell_k=s_k+\tau_k,\quad a_k=\ell_k+\mathrm{RTT},
\end{split}
\label{eq:flight}
\end{equation}
where $k=0,\dots,N-1$ is the zero-based segment index, $c$ is the window at the start of the flight, $\tau_k$ is the serialization time of segment $k$ and $\ell_{-1}=0$. The floor operator $\lfloor\cdot\rfloor$ releases two queued segments per returned ACK in slow start. \Cref{eq:flight} controls the slow-start waiting inside a flight. The last byte arrives at $\ell_{N-1}+\mathrm{RTT}/2$ under a symmetric one-way delay. The recurrence costs $\mathcal{O}(N)$ operations per flight. The model excludes ACK serialization, receive-window limits, packet loss and queueing from other flows.

The implementation-specific model represents two observed behaviors. First, the tested OpenSSL configuration writes handshake data through a 4\,096-byte buffer and writes the application request separately. With \texttt{TCP\_NODELAY}, a partial segment at a write boundary leaves the sender when the window permits it. The model accounts for these boundaries and coalesces queued tails when the window is exhausted. Second, the model updates the next flight window using Linux-style slow-start validation. In the inspected kernel headers, \texttt{tcp\_is\_cwnd\_limited()} first accepts an explicitly window-limited sender. In slow start, it then tests whether \texttt{snd\_cwnd} is below twice \texttt{max\_packets\_out}, the tracked maximum of outstanding packets~\cite{linuxtcp}. This test uses the tracked maximum. It does not use the instantaneous number of packets in flight. The model tracks the peak number of outstanding segments during ACK replay and updates the window between flights using this value. A short flight that uses less than half of the available window does not enlarge it in this abstraction. The source inspection covers Linux~6.11 and 6.14. We empirically examined the behavior of the testbed kernel (Linux~7.0); \cref{sec:mech} reports a plain-TCP measurement of the window on this kernel. RFC~7661 specifies a related congestion-window validation method for rate-limited traffic~\cite{rfc7661}. The model follows the inspected Linux rule and does not implement RFC~7661.

The idealized closed-form model is the baseline of this study. It uses $\lceil\text{bytes}/\mathrm{MSS}\rceil$ segments per flight, where $\lceil\cdot\rceil$ is the ceiling operator, and it enlarges the window by the segment count after each flight. The implementation-specific model keeps the ACK-clock recurrence of \cref{eq:flight} within each flight. It abstracts the observed stack and does not execute the complete Linux TCP state machine.

The model decomposes the latency of a transaction as
\begin{equation}
T=T_\cpu+T_\mathrm{ser}+T_\mathrm{prop}+T_\mathrm{ssw}+T_\mathrm{pen},
\label{eq:latency}
\end{equation}
where $T_\cpu$ is cryptographic computation, $T_\mathrm{ser}$ is serialization, $T_\mathrm{prop}$ is propagation, $T_\mathrm{ssw}$ is slow-start waiting and $T_\mathrm{pen}$ is an optional added middlebox delay. \Cref{eq:latency} is exact for the serial model. Real processing can overlap network reception. The size-dependent transport overhead is
\begin{equation}
O=T_\mathrm{ser}+T_\mathrm{ssw}+T_\mathrm{pen}.
\label{eq:overhead}
\end{equation}
\Cref{eq:overhead} excludes $T_\mathrm{prop}$, since propagation is common to the compared suites. The comparison of $O$ with $T_\cpu$ states whether a transaction is transport-bound or computation-bound.

The optional delay term is $T_\mathrm{pen}=\pi\,\bigl|\{f:N_f>\theta\}\bigr|$, where $N_f$ is the segment count of flight $f$, $|\cdot|$ denotes set cardinality, $\pi$ is the added delay in milliseconds and $\theta$ is the segment threshold. The term represents a hypothetical size-threshold delay. It is not a measured law of middlebox processing. The reference value is $\pi=0$; larger values are sensitivity inputs. Reported connection failures with large handshakes~\cite{westerbaan2021} motivate the examination of size-related constraints. They do not calibrate $\pi$, and the model does not represent connection failures. Size outputs use KiB ($1\,\mathrm{KiB}=1024$\,bytes). Bit rates use decimal Mbit/s.

For certificate compression~\cite{rfc8879}, the model treats keys and signatures as incompressible. The compression ratio of DER metadata is 0.68 for the first certificate and 0.52 for each subsequent certificate (calibrated on five public bank and payment chains). The assumed client decompression rates are 330, 33, 3.3 and 0.33\,MB/s. These rates are sensitivity inputs. They are not measurements of embedded clients.

\subsection{Parameter space and reported quantities}
The main sweep contains 140\,400 cold-connection rows and 35\,100 warm-connection rows, for a total of 175\,500 rows. The cold rows form the full factorial of thirteen suites, six profiles, four chain depths, six bit rates (10, 100, 200, 400, 800 and 1\,600\Mbits), five RTTs (5, 12, 30, 50 and 100\ms), three MTUs (1\,280, 1\,500 and 9\,000\,bytes) and five assumed delays $\pi\in\{0,3,6,12,24\}$\ms. Warm rows transmit no chain and have a single depth state. The threshold is $\theta=4$ segments; a separate sweep uses $\theta\in\{2,4,8,16\}$.

Unless stated otherwise, individual predictions use 100\Mbits, a 30\ms RTT, an MTU of 1\,500\,bytes, $\pi=0$ and a cold connection. The baseline suite is ECDSA-P256 with X25519. The share of transport-bound rows is
\begin{equation}
\phi=\frac{\bigl|\{j:O_j>T_{\cpu,j}\}\bigr|}{J},
\label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
where $j$ indexes the $J$ equally weighted cold rows of the selected grid. $\phi$ is a descriptive fraction of the chosen grid. It is not an estimate of the probability of bank traffic. The grid with all $\pi$ values includes hypothetical nonzero delays. \Cref{S-tab:protocols} lists the protocol reference depths and fixed object sizes.

\subsection{Measured inputs}
\label{sec:meas}
\paragraph{Primitive costs} The Arm primitive benchmarks and object-size measurements ran on an NVIDIA GX10 (10 Cortex-X925 and 10 Cortex-A725 cores; Linux~6.14). A C harness measured per-operation costs via the high-level envelope (EVP) interface of OpenSSL~3.5.4~\cite{openssl35}, which the OpenSSL TLS stack uses. It covers RSA with probabilistic signature scheme (RSA-PSS) padding, ECDSA, X25519, P-256, ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA-SHA2. liboqs~0.14.0~\cite{stebila2016} provided Falcon. Each value is the median of seven 0.5\,s runs pinned to one core. The minimum and maximum of the seven runs lie within 0.6\% of the median for the median operation and within 6\% for the noisiest operation, ML-DSA-87 signing (\cref{S-tab:cpu}). ML-DSA-65 signing costs 0.59\ms with OpenSSL and 0.27\ms with liboqs. SLH-DSA-128s signing takes 171\ms. The efficiency core is about 2 times slower than the performance core (1.5 to 2.4 across operations, \cref{S-tab:cpu}).

To anchor the CPU scale on a second architecture, we repeated the measurement on one core of an Intel Core i7-5600U (Broadwell, 2.5\,GHz, AVX2, turbo disabled). This machine hosts the testbed of \cref{sec:testbed}. Elliptic-curve and lattice operations are 2.2 to 4.4 times slower than on the X925 (median 3.15 for signatures and 3.87 for key establishment). This core is about 1.4 times slower than the Cortex-A725. RSA signing is 1.7 times slower. The hash-based SLH-DSA is about 8 times slower, since this core has no SHA instruction extensions (\cref{S-tab:cpux86}). Two measurements on different cores of an idle machine agree within 4\% for signatures. The CPU scale of 4 in the sweeps corresponds to a measured x86 core. The CPU scale of 16 is an assumed processing slowdown and not an HSM measurement.

\paragraph{Certificates and handshakes} We generated DER hierarchies with a root, 0--3 intermediates and server and client leaves with bank-like extensions for RSA, ECDSA, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA. We recorded 56 configurations of real TLS~1.3 handshakes with OpenSSL \texttt{s\_server}/\texttt{s\_client} (21 handshakes each, depth 1--4, with and without mTLS) through a loopback relay that counts the bytes of each flight. OpenSSL~3.5 has no TLS code points for SLH-DSA or Falcon. Certificates and CPU measurements cover these schemes.

\paragraph{Application objects} We produced real signed application objects with OpenSSL: JWS client assertions and access tokens, DPoP proofs with the key in the JSON Object Signing and Encryption (JOSE) header and an XMLDSig signature over a pacs.008 document with the signer certificate in \texttt{KeyInfo}. We compared each object with the model.

\subsection{Implementation verification and input checks}
The test suites check byte accounting, latency-component sums and selected monotonicity properties. An independently implemented SimPy discrete-event scheduler reproduces the transport outputs across 126\,360 flows with a maximum absolute latency difference of $1.3\times10^{-11}$\ms. This comparison verifies the consistency of two implementations of the same assumptions. It does not validate the assumptions against a physical network. The full event-driven grid uses the implementation-specific model; an additional 20\,000-flow sample checks the idealized model.

With certificate metadata calibrated on the generated ECDSA chain (854 bytes per leaf and 663 per CA certificate; the sweeps use 900 and 520, within the range varied in \cref{sec:sens}), the predicted sizes of the generated certificates differ by at most 0.8\% for the measured RSA, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA certificates (\cref{S-tab:certs}). The modeled TLS flights match the corresponding OpenSSL handshakes with a median size error of 0.02\% and a maximum of 0.40\% (\cref{S-tab:tls}). Measured JWS, DPoP and XMLDSig objects provide further size checks (\cref{S-tab:app}): the model overstates the JWS and DPoP objects by 130 to 250 bytes (41--45\% for ECDSA-P256, 22--29\% for RSA-2048, at most 6.3\% for the post-quantum suites) and the XMLDSig document by at most 21 bytes. A check on two public servers (\cref{S-tab:internet}) found that X25519MLKEM768 adds 2.9 and 2.5\ms (95\% intervals [1.2, 4.4] and [0.1, 5.7]\ms) at RTTs of 30 and 34\ms, about a tenth of an RTT or less; it is an input check, not a validation. These checks concern byte construction. They do not establish complete protocol interoperability.

The loopback handshake-time residual after subtraction of the modeled primitive time is $-0.1$ to 1.1\ms at the 10th--90th percentiles. With the measured x86 costs, the modeled CPU steps differ from 24 unshaped transaction timings by an MAE of 1.52\ms; the mean error (model minus measurement) is $-1.14$\ms. These residuals contain library and local-stack work that primitive benchmarks do not capture. In 3 of the 14 handshakes of \cref{S-tab:tls} the modeled CPU time exceeds the measured loopback time (by up to 0.62\ms, ML-DSA-87 at depth 4), consistent with a serial model that does not overlap operations. \Cref{sec:testbed} reports the network validation.

\section{Experimental validation and transport mechanisms}
\label{sec:testbed}
A client that sends an ML-DSA-65 authentication flight one segment above the available window waits one additional RTT. This section identifies, on real transactions, the stack behaviors that the model must contain to locate these waits, and it measures the prediction error. The identification grid and the later measurements at other bit rates, MTUs and initial windows are evaluated separately.

\subsection{Testbed}
We replayed real transactions over an emulated link. A virtual Ethernet pair joins two Linux network namespaces. The \texttt{tc netem} network emulator adds a one-way delay of RTT/2 in each direction, a rate limit and the MTU. The link uses IPv6 with TCP timestamps disabled. The headers occupy the $40+20$\,bytes of the model ($\mathrm{MSS}=\mathrm{MTU}-60$\,bytes). Segmentation offload is disabled, and each segment is one packet. The sender uses Reno slow start with quick acknowledgments and a per-route initial window. This configuration is the closest Linux configuration to \cref{eq:flight}.

A C program built on OpenSSL~3.5 opens a new TCP connection and performs a full TLS~1.3 handshake with mutual authentication. It uses the real DER chains of \cref{sec:meas} without session tickets or a session cache. It sends a 302-byte request and receives a 222-byte response, the ISO~8583 authorization profile. One experiment uses SWIFT-sized requests of 10.9\,KiB and 3.6\,KiB. Client and server run on separate physical cores of an Intel Core i7-5600U (two cores, four threads; Linux~7.0.0-42, iproute2~6.1, HAProxy~2.8.30). The shaped measurements contain 20 repetitions in 144 configurations, 30 in 72 and 50--80 in ten after pooling; the runner discards three warm-up transactions. The CPU clock of the testbed host was fixed for the campaigns of the identification and validation sets: the runner recorded the machine state at the start of each run, and the snapshots of these campaigns show the \texttt{performance} governor and disabled turbo (\texttt{intel\_no\_turbo}\,=\,1), set with the repository script \texttt{host\_tune.sh}. The runs with other TCP settings (CUBIC, delayed acknowledgments, timestamps, BBR) were repeated with the same fixed clock; the first session (\texttt{ondemand} governor, turbo enabled; archived in the repository) gave MAEs within 0.08\ms for Reno and CUBIC and 5.47 against 3.51\ms for BBR. The kernel TCP counters (\texttt{TCP\_INFO}) report the segment size, the initial window and the number of data segments of each transaction.

\subsection{Metric definition}
The excess latency $E_j$ of configuration $j$ is the median time of the shaped configuration minus the median time of an unshaped run with the same suite, depth and authentication mode. The model prediction is $\hat{E}_j=T_j-T_{\cpu,j}$. The subtraction removes common primitive, library and local-stack costs. The cancellation is approximate, since scheduling and overlap can differ between shaped and unshaped runs. The error is $e_j=\hat{E}_j-E_j$ (model minus measurement). The accuracy metrics over $M$ configurations are
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}|e_j|,\qquad
\mathrm{RMSE}=\Bigl(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}e_j^2\Bigr)^{1/2},\qquad
\bar{e}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}e_j,
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
where RMSE is the root-mean-square error and $\bar{e}$ is the bias. Each error uses configuration-level medians.

\subsection{Comparison design and observed mechanisms}
\label{sec:mech}
The retained direct-path dataset contains 5\,660 successful shaped transactions and 1\,300 unshaped baselines, 6\,960 transactions in total. The comparison covers 226 configurations: six suites, chain depth 1--4, RTT of 5--100\ms, bit rates of 10, 100 and 1\,000\Mbits, MTUs of 1\,280, 1\,500 and 9\,000\,bytes and initial windows of 10 and 32 segments. The six suites use the real DER chains of \cref{sec:meas} with mutual authentication: RSA-2048 and ECDSA-P256 with classical key exchange; ML-DSA-44, ML-DSA-65 and ML-DSA-87 with ML-KEM; and ECDSA-P256 with the hybrid X25519MLKEM768 group. The hybrid group uses code point 0x11EC, registered by RFC~10024. OpenSSL~3.5.4 does not use the pre-standard Kyber code points. The ML-DSA suites use the pure groups MLKEM768 and MLKEM1024, which RFC~10024 does not define; an Internet-Draft in the RFC Editor queue defines them~\cite{tlsmlkem}. The ML-DSA signature schemes of the OpenSSL TLS stack follow an Internet-Draft~\cite{tlsmldsa}. Falcon-512, SLH-DSA and the composite suites cannot run in OpenSSL~3.5 TLS. Their results rest on the model and on measured certificate sizes and CPU costs. The requests are ISO~8583-sized (SWIFT-sized in one experiment). The testbed validates the stack's behavior, not bank traffic. The TLS bytes read and written by the client lie within 0.95--1.09 times the modeled bytes of the whole transaction. The measured bytes exceed the modeled bytes by a median of 127\,bytes (maximum 763\,bytes), which we did not itemize, whereas the modeled handshake flights differ from the OpenSSL handshakes by at most 0.40\% (\cref{sec:meas}). The segment count, which decides the round trips, was checked directly against the kernel counters below.

\Cref{fig:testbed,tab:testbed} compare the transport time. Panel (a) of \cref{fig:testbed} shows the idealized model, and panel (b) shows the stack model with the two mechanisms below. The idealized model has an MAE of 4.70\ms and a maximum error of 97.1\ms. Its error exceeds 0.4\,RTT in 21 configurations. In 11 of them, the error is about one RTT, and the largest error is 1.9\,RTT. Two stack mechanisms explain these errors.

\emph{Window validation.} The idealized model enlarges the window of a direction by the segments of every flight. In this model, the one-segment ClientHello enlarges the client window to 11, enough for the 11-segment client flight of this example. The observed Linux stack does not enlarge the window after this short flight, and the client flight pays one additional RTT. For ML-DSA-65 at depth~2, an MTU of 1\,500\,bytes and a 30\ms RTT, the testbed measures 148.9\ms. The idealized model predicts 121.8\ms; the model with window validation predicts 150.7\ms.

\emph{Segmentation.} The kernel data-segment counters show more segments per flight than $\lceil\text{bytes}/\mathrm{MSS}\rceil$. A system-call trace (\texttt{strace}) of OpenSSL shows the cause. Handshake messages leave through a 4\,096-byte buffer; for ML-DSA-65 at depth~1, the server writes 4\,096, 4\,096 and 2\,688\,bytes. Sosnowski et~al.\ report the same buffer in a patched OpenSSL~\cite{sosnowski2023}. The request is a separate write. With \texttt{TCP\_NODELAY}, a write boundary generates a partial segment when the window permits immediate transmission. Neither mechanism suffices alone (\cref{S-tab:ablation}): the MAE is 3.34\ms with window validation only, 3.15\ms with the write boundaries only and 1.65\ms with both, against 4.70\ms for the idealized model. The idealized model predicts the exact segment count in 78 of 300 flights (the two directions of each of 150 configurations with kernel counters). The model with this write pattern predicts 237 of them. In the remaining flights, the measured flight almost always contains one additional segment, mostly with the larger initial window, and the latency of these runs does not change. The effect matters when a flight lies just below the window. ML-DSA-65 at depth~1 and an MTU of 1\,280\,bytes has nine segments in each of its two long flights in the idealized model and eleven to twelve in the measurement. It pays two RTTs more than predicted: 147.3\ms measured, 91.9\ms in the idealized model and 150.4\ms with the write pattern.

\emph{Window rule measured without TLS.} A separate plain-TCP experiment on the same kernel (a fresh connection, a burst of $n$ full segments, a reply that acknowledges all of it, then the window read from \texttt{TCP\_INFO}) measured the window after an $n$-segment flight as $\max(w_0,\min(w_0+n,2n))$ in 12 of 13 tested configurations (data in the public repository), for the tested RTTs, rates, initial windows ($w_0$ in the formula), delayed acknowledgments, CUBIC, timestamps and offload. The window rule of the model, derived from the replay of the ACK clock, differs from this closed form by one segment at odd $n>w_0$; none of the 238 full-handshake configurations measured (the 226 above and 12 with larger requests) is affected. At an MTU of 9\,000\,bytes, the measured window deviates from the closed form for $n\ge 8$. The advertised receive window is then about 63\,000\,bytes, seven segments, and the sender is limited by it: with the receive buffer raised from 128\,KiB to 1\,MiB, 16 of 20 flight sizes follow the closed form (7 of 20 by default). The other four stay one to two segments below it.

\subsection{Prediction accuracy and residual errors}
With the two mechanisms, the MAE is 1.65\ms, the bias is $+0.10$\ms, the maximum error is 5.4\ms. The model lies within 1\ms of the measurement in 110 of 226 configurations. The maximum error is 3.9, 5.4 and 3.8\ms at RTTs of 5, 30 and 100\ms. Since the maximum error does not grow with the RTT, it is consistent with residual processing or timing offsets. An omitted RTT step would scale with the RTT. The measurement is more precise than the model: the model lies inside the 95\% bootstrap interval of the measured median in 46 of 226 configurations, which indicates a systematic residual beyond measurement uncertainty. Processing overlap is a candidate cause that we did not isolate. The RMSE is 2.16\ms. The MAE equals 1.2\% of the mean excess latency, and its 95\% bootstrap interval is 1.48--1.83\ms (\cref{S-tab:residuals,S-fig:residuals}).

\emph{What the error measures.} The two questions the model answers should be read separately. For the round-trip steps, the idealized model is wrong by more than half an RTT and more than 5\ms in 19 of the 226 configurations (errors of 15 to 97\ms), the stack model in none; its largest error is 5.4\ms, three configurations exceed 5\ms and none exceeds 10\ms. The four configurations above 0.4\,RTT all lie at a 5\ms RTT, where that bound is 2\ms. For the absolute latency, the accuracy is of the order of one to two milliseconds with a systematic component: the model is within 1\ms in 110 of 226 configurations and inside the measurement interval in 46, and the MAE ranges from 0.64 to 2.14\ms across suites and from 0.96 to 2.04\ms between the core grid and the later campaign (\cref{tab:testbed,S-tab:residuals}). We therefore read the model as locating the round-trip steps reliably on the tested stack, not as predicting every latency to the millisecond, and the overall MAE is not a uniform accuracy.

\emph{Scope at an MTU of 9\,000 bytes.} The stack mechanisms do not improve the prediction there: the MAE of the 48 configurations is 2.17\ms for the stack model and 2.11\ms for the idealized one, against 1.87 and 8.22\ms at 1\,280 and 1.33 and 4.03\ms at 1\,500 bytes. The model has no receive-window limit, which limits plain TCP at this MTU (\cref{sec:mech}); we did not model it and do not show that it explains the TLS errors, which also coincide in part with the initial window of 32 segments. The validation therefore supports the model at MTUs of 1\,280 and 1\,500 bytes (178 configurations, MAE 1.51\ms) and does not at 9\,000 bytes.

\emph{Location of the error.} No parameter of the stack model was fitted to these measurements. The two behaviors were identified on the core grid of 82 configurations at 100\Mbits and an initial window of 10 segments; this grid contains the runs on which the behaviors were found. The model is structural with $\pi=0$. The 144 configurations that vary the bit rate, the MTU and the initial window come from a later measurement campaign. The MAE is 0.96\ms on the core grid and 2.04\ms off the grid. The absolute MAE is 0.92--1.84\ms across the three RTTs. The relative error is largest at a 5\ms RTT (4.9\% of the excess latency) and smallest at 100\ms (0.2\%). The error depends on the setting (\cref{S-tab:residuals}): the MAE is 2.35\ms at an initial window of 32 segments against 1.32\ms at 10, and 2.17\ms at an MTU of 9\,000\,bytes against 1.33\ms at 1\,500\,bytes. It does not depend on the chain depth (1.58--1.72\ms). Of the 226 configurations, 178 use a 30\ms RTT. The bias is $-1.5$\ms for ECDSA-P256 with X25519, $-0.6$\ms for the hybrid suite, $+0.9$\ms for RSA-2048 and $+0.7$ to $+1.0$\ms for the ML-DSA suites. The model overstates the ML-DSA increment over the ECDSA baseline by 2.2--2.5\ms. The largest error (5.4\ms; ML-DSA-65, depth~2, 30\ms RTT, MTU of 9\,000\,bytes, initial window of 32 segments) is a fraction of one RTT. It occurs at an MTU of 9\,000\,bytes, where the receive window limits plain TCP (\cref{sec:mech}); the model has no receive-window limit, and we do not claim that this explains the error. Computation that overlaps the network transfer remains a candidate cause of these offsets.

\subsection{Additional network and TCP settings}
The 144 off-grid configurations vary the bit rate (10 and 1\,000\Mbits), the MTU (up to 9\,000\,bytes) and the initial window (32 segments). On this set, the idealized model has an MAE of 4.32\ms, and 11 configurations exceed 0.4\,RTT. The stack model has an MAE of 2.04\ms and a maximum error of 5.4\ms. No configuration exceeds 0.4\,RTT. Its sign follows the bit rate: the bias is $+1.1$\ms at 10\Mbits and $-1.2$\ms at 1\,000\Mbits. We did not trace this dependence to a single cause; the accounting of the rate limiter is one candidate.

\Cref{S-tab:variants} reports other TCP settings. With CUBIC~\cite{rfc9438} in place of Reno (216 configurations), the MAE is 1.19\ms (idealized: 5.43\ms). Delayed acknowledgments, TCP timestamps ($\mathrm{MSS}=\mathrm{MTU}-72$\,bytes) and both (72 configurations) give 1.26, 1.32 and 1.23\ms (idealized: about 11\ms); their excess latency differs from the Reno quick-acknowledgment run by a median of at most 0.15\ms. Every configuration above 0.4\,RTT lies at a 5\ms RTT, and the largest error (3.5--5.1\ms) is similar at 5, 30 and 100\ms, as expected of offsets and not of an omitted RTT step. The small effect of delayed acknowledgments is consistent with Linux starting each connection in quick-acknowledgment mode, which we did not verify. Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time (BBR) congestion control lies outside the model. In an exploratory run of 8 configurations that cross a window boundary, the model under-predicted BBR by an MAE of 3.51\ms (maximum 21.8\ms, at a 100\ms RTT); this set calibrates nothing and supports no ranking of controllers.

\subsection{Measured TCP-proxy effect}
The term $T_\mathrm{pen}$ of \cref{eq:latency} applies only when a middlebox delays long flights. On the direct path, the least-squares fit gives $\pi=0.00$\ms at $\theta=1$ segment. Its RMSE is 2.16\ms, equal to the RMSE at $\pi=0$. The threshold-delay term does not improve the fit. We then inserted HAProxy~2.8 as a TCP proxy between the two namespaces in two modes. In the first mode, the proxy forwards bytes on arrival. In the second mode, it waits for the complete ClientHello before routing. The test covers 16 configurations: ECDSA-P256, ML-DSA-44, ML-DSA-65 and ML-DSA-87 at chain depths 1--4, a 30\ms RTT, 100\Mbits, an MTU of 1\,500\,bytes and an initial window of 10 segments, with flights of 2--27 segments. The proxy adds a mean of 0.18\ms (range $-0.22$ to 0.31\ms) in the first mode and 0.28\ms (range $-0.50$ to 0.55\ms) in the second mode. No large size-dependent delay appears in this sample. The check covers one proxy and one version. The swept values of $\pi$ up to 24\ms represent untested devices such as deep packet inspection, TLS-terminating proxies or hardware firewalls. The measurements support $\pi=0$, which the single values below use.

\subsection{Authentication-byte composition}
The purpose of this analysis is to locate the bytes that post-quantum authentication adds. The configuration is a cold transaction at each protocol's reference depth. ML-DSA-65 with ML-KEM-768 enlarges a cold SWIFT~MX transaction from 23.0 to 83.4\,KiB. Falcon-512 yields 39.9\,KiB and SLH-DSA-128f yields 246.7\,KiB. \Cref{fig:sizes} shows the composition for SWIFT~MX at depth~3 and FAPI~2.0 at depth~2. For ML-DSA-65, certificate chains account for 42\% of the bytes, XMLDSig signatures and embedded certificates for 30\%, \texttt{CertificateVerify} for 8\% and the payment payload for 17\%; key shares and framing make up the other 3\%. Lean protocols grow most. ISO~8583 grows by a factor of 6.8 with ML-DSA-65, since its handshake chains dwarf a 300-byte authorization. ISO~20022 grows 4.4 times and FAPI~2.0 grows 5.7 times (\cref{S-tab:sizes}). The hybrid key share adds 2.2\,KiB. On a warm connection, ISO~8583 does not grow, since it uses a symmetric MAC. ISO~20022 grows 3.1 times and FAPI~2.0 grows 4.8 times on a warm connection, since their application-layer signatures remain.

\subsection{Chain depth and round-trip thresholds}
\Cref{fig:depth} shows SWIFT~MX latency against chain depth for three MTUs at 100\Mbits, a 30\ms RTT and $\pi=0$, on a logarithmic scale. The latency grows in whole RTTs. The classical baseline gains $+29.6$\ms from depth 1 to 4, about one RTT. From depth~2, its client flight, a handshake flight followed by a separately written request of about 11\,KiB, exceeds the initial window of ten segments. ML-DSA-65 gains $+60.0$\ms, two RTTs. Slow-start waiting contributes 56.5\ms, serialization 2.9\ms and computation 0.6\ms. The post-quantum-specific cost of three simultaneous depth increments is the difference, 30.5\ms (computed from unrounded values), or about 10.2\ms per simultaneous chain-depth increment. We define the average increment as $\bar{\Delta}_d=(T_{d=4}-T_{d=1})/3$. It averages three increments at the two peers. It is not the marginal cost of one certificate. For ML-DSA-65 at an MTU of 1\,500\,bytes, $\bar{\Delta}_d=20.0$\ms in absolute terms (3.3\ms at a 5\ms RTT and 66.7\ms at a 100\ms RTT). Jumbo frames (MTU of 9\,000\,bytes) reduce it to 1.1\ms. A delay of $\pi=24$\ms raises the jumbo-frame value to 9.1\ms (\cref{S-tab:depth,S-tab:interaction,S-tab:threshold}). An initial window of 32 segments halves the depth effect to $+29.9$\ms. The two chains contribute separately (\cref{S-tab:asym}): growing only the server chain from depth 1 to 4 adds 30.7\ms and only the client chain 29.3\ms, which sum to the 60.0\ms. Each chain pays one RTT, at different depths, since each direction has its own window. The size of the chain comes mainly from the CA keys and signatures: in a model-only scenario with a post-quantum leaf key under classical CAs, the depth effect almost disappears ($+0.7$\ms), and with a classical leaf under post-quantum CAs it is $+32.4$\ms (\cref{S-tab:mixed}). Only the first case authenticates the whole chain with post-quantum signatures; the others are what a staged migration might look like and are not secure against a quantum adversary where the classical algorithm remains.

The location of each flight depends on the stack (\cref{sec:testbed}). The idealized model produces three artifacts. It makes the hybrid suite faster than the baseline at an MTU of 1\,280\,bytes (92 vs.\ 121\ms). It gives the composite suite $+32.8$\ms against $+59.9$\ms for ML-DSA-65. It predicts a flat classical baseline ($+0.6$\ms). The stack model removes these artifacts. The hybrid suite is not faster than the baseline (122 vs.\ 121\ms); the testbed measures 121.2 vs.\ 121.0\ms on SWIFT-sized transactions. The composite suite follows ML-DSA-65 ($+60.4$ vs.\ $+60.0$\ms), and the baseline pays one RTT. ML-DSA-87 shows a smaller depth effect than ML-DSA-65 ($+32.3$ vs.\ $+60.0$\ms) in the two models, since its flights already cross an additional window boundary at depth~1. The ML-DSA-65 depth effect of about 60\ms does not depend on these details, but the classical baseline's does: with larger certificates (leaf 1\,400, CA 800 bytes) its chain already crosses the window at depth 1 and its depth effect falls from 29.6 to 0.7\ms, so the post-quantum-specific difference of 30.5\ms is conditional on the certificate metadata (\cref{sec:sens,S-tab:sens}). RSA-2048 behaves alike ($+0.6$\ms): its largest flight has 12 segments at depth 1, so it already pays the round trip that the baseline pays from depth 2.

\subsection{Transport overhead and modeled cryptographic time}
The purpose of this comparison is to decide whether the size-dependent transport overhead $O$ or the modeled cryptographic primitives $T_\cpu$ dominate the added latency. $T_\cpu$ is the sum of the primitive operations on the serial critical path of the model. It excludes HSMs, middleware, queues, databases and backend processing, so a share above one half does not mean that the network takes more time than the gateway as a whole. The metric is $\phi$ of \cref{eq:share}, a fraction of equally weighted grid rows. With $\pi=0$, $O>T_\cpu$ holds in 44.2\% of the baseline rows and in 75.6\% of the ML-DSA-65 rows. With all assumed delays, the shares are 51\% and 87\% (\cref{tab:master}). Nonzero delays are hypothetical sensitivity inputs; the HAProxy measurements do not support them.

With the measured x86 primitive costs, the all-delay shares are 38\% for the baseline, 66\% for Falcon-512 and 78\% for ML-DSA-65, close to the results at CPU scale 4. The serial model charges all primitive steps on its critical path; an assumed overlap that removes half or three-quarters of this charge raises the all-delay ML-DSA-65 share to 91\% and 94\%. This is an arithmetic sensitivity check, not a measurement of overlap.

\Cref{fig:ratio} shows the ratio of transport overhead to cryptographic time for the reference SWIFT~MX profile with $\pi=0$. Above 100\Mbits, the ratio changes little with the bit rate. The remaining ACK waiting depends on flight size, window state and RTT. SLH-DSA-128s is the main computation-bound exception. Its four online signatures contribute most of the 685\ms primitive budget per transaction. SLH-DSA-128f produces larger authentication traffic. The result concerns the selected implementations and protocol operations. It excludes backend payment processing.

\subsection{Certificate compression and decompression cost}
Keys and signatures are pseudorandom and do not compress. Compression removes 31\% of an ECDSA-P256 chain and 5\% of an ML-DSA-65 chain. It saves at least 1\ms in only 5--21\% of the configurations. The gain depends on whether compression moves a flight below a window boundary. For ML-DSA-65, the maximum saving on this grid is about one RTT. Other suites exceed this bound (\cref{S-tab:rq4}). Decompression can cost more than compression saves. At an assumed client decompression rate of 3.3\,MB/s, compression is net-harmful in 95\% of the ML-DSA-65 configurations and in 0\% of the ECDSA-P256 configurations. The median break-even decompression rate for ML-DSA-65 is about 13\,MB/s (\cref{S-tab:rq4,S-fig:cc}). Zlib at level 9 reduces real ML-DSA and SLH-DSA chains to 0.88--0.98 of their size, within 0.05 of the modeled ratio (\cref{S-tab:comp_real}).

\subsection{Sensitivity to model inputs}
\label{sec:sens}
We varied the certificate metadata (600--1\,400\,bytes per leaf), the XMLDSig certificate embedding and the window of reused connections (\cref{S-tab:sens}). Omission of the signer certificate from XMLDSig reduces the ML-DSA-65 SWIFT~MX transaction from 83.4 to 67.2\,KiB. A reused connection that keeps a large window ($c=64$) reduces the warm ML-DSA-65 ISO~20022 exchange from 92 to 34\ms (baseline: 31\ms). Individual RTT steps move with these parameters. The reference comparisons keep the order Falcon-512 $<$ ML-DSA-65 $<$ SLH-DSA-128f in the evaluated input variants; this order compares selected parameter sets and is not universal over security categories or stacks.

A variance-based global sensitivity analysis over the full factorial designs computes first- and second-order indices (\cref{S-tab:sobol}). The RTT explains 0.59 of the variance of log latency, and the suite explains 0.28. For the post-quantum factor of the lattice schemes, the MTU explains 0.18, the RTT 0.20 and the suite 0.16.

\subsection{Assumption-based uncertainty analysis}
\label{sec:rq5}
The deterministic model propagates selected input uncertainty. Structural model error and unmeasured deployment conditions remain outside this analysis. We drew 4\,000 samples of five inputs with fixed seed 1 (\cref{S-tab:priors}): the certificate metadata (600--1\,400\,bytes per leaf), the CPU scale (log-uniform on $[1,16]$, an assumed range of primitive-processing slowdowns), $\pi\in[0,24]$\ms, $\theta\in[2,16]$ segments and the initial window (10, 32 or 64 segments with probabilities 0.6, 0.3 and 0.1). For each sample, we recomputed a SWIFT~MX transaction at depth~2, 100\Mbits and a 30\ms RTT (\cref{S-tab:uncertainty}). The priors are assumptions, not fitted distributions, so the resulting intervals are conditional on them and are not confidence intervals for the performance of any bank. They exclude structural uncertainty from other operating systems, TLS libraries and middleboxes, jitter, packet loss and institution-specific traffic.

The order Falcon-512 $<$ ML-DSA-65 $<$ SLH-DSA-128f holds in 100\% of the draws. Size-dependent transport overhead exceeds the modeled primitive time in 91\% of the draws: 99\% for CPU scales below 4 and 73\% for scales of 12--16. The ML-DSA-65 latency factor relative to the baseline has a median of 1.46 (90\% interval: 1.19--1.72). The Falcon-512 factor has a median of 1.05 (1.01--1.23). We measure input influence with the Spearman rank correlation coefficient $\rho$. For the factor, the CPU scale ($\rho=0.54$) and $\pi$ ($\rho=0.47$) dominate the initial window ($\rho=-0.36$). For $\bar{\Delta}_d$, the initial window dominates ($\rho=-0.74$). The average of about 20\ms per simultaneous ML-DSA-65 depth increment holds for the default window of 10 segments (median 22.5\ms, 90\% interval 20.1--28.4\ms). With 32 segments, the median is 12.4\ms (10.1--18.2\ms). With 64 segments, it is 3.4\ms (1.3--9.5\ms). The depth cost depends on the initial window. It is not a universal per-certificate constant. The testbed found no delay for a TCP proxy (\cref{sec:testbed}), and the intervals with nonzero $\pi$ contain hypothetical behavior of untested devices. With $\pi$ fixed at 0 and the other draws unchanged, the size-dependent overhead exceeds the modeled primitive time in 71\% of the draws (80\% for CPU scales below 4 and 59\% for 12--16), the ML-DSA-65 factor has a median of 1.33 (90\% interval 1.09--1.54), the Falcon-512 factor 1.02 (1.01--1.03), the order of the three schemes still holds in 100\% of the draws, and the median depth cost per simultaneous increment is 20.7, 10.7 and 1.8\ms at initial windows of 10, 32 and 64 segments. The 91\% above therefore depends on the hypothetical $\pi$.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Contribution and deployment implications}
A gateway operator who plans an ML-DSA-65 migration needs the round trips that each authentication object adds. The empirical contribution of this study is the reduction of configuration-level excess-latency error, from 4.70\ms to 1.65\ms MAE, after the model accounts for write boundaries and window validation. Prior work identified authentication size and the initial window as the relevant factors; the present measurements connect them to the individual flights that incur additional ACK waiting. The MAE of 2.04\ms on the later off-grid configurations supports the abstraction over the tested bit rates, MTUs and initial windows, and the remaining systematic error limits the interpretation of predicted differences of a few milliseconds.

The application layer matters. mTLS certificate chains account for 42\% of the reference ML-DSA-65 SWIFT~MX bytes and signed application objects with embedded certificates for 30\%. A reference to the signer certificate reduces the modeled transaction from 83.4 to 67.2\,KiB, and a retained larger window reduces the modeled warm ISO~20022 exchange from 92 to 34\ms. Deployment decisions therefore start from the actual certificate path, application envelope and connection lifecycle. Operators can remove unnecessary transmitted certificates and redundant embedding where trust and protocol requirements allow. On controlled paths, a larger initial window or MTU can remove RTT steps, subject to checks of path support, congestion effects and other flows. The study establishes no universally best intervention and no maximum acceptable chain depth.

Signature selection depends on security category and implementation constraints. Falcon-512 has smaller modeled authentication traffic than ML-DSA-65, but the two do not form an equal-security comparison. SLH-DSA-128s is computation-bound under the measured primitive implementation, and SLH-DSA-128f produces substantially more traffic. The SLH-DSA figures that involve messages above 16\,KB depend on an unvalidated write pattern (Supplementary Appendix~\ref{S-app:records}), so we draw no conclusion from them about the order of the depth effects of the schemes; the order of their total latencies is unchanged. These results quantify performance costs for the evaluated parameters. They do not establish deployment readiness or an exclusive role for hash-based signatures. \Cref{S-tab:status} records the specifications and measured components.

\subsection{Evidence boundaries and limitations}
The main empirical comparison uses one Linux/OpenSSL stack, a single x86 host with isolated network namespaces, no configured packet loss and no cross traffic. The testbed validates excess latency for request-response probes, mainly with ISO~8583-sized messages and one SWIFT-sized comparison. It does not validate ISO~20022 or FAPI workflows against operational services. The runs with other TCP settings do not establish transferability to other operating systems, TLS libraries or kernels. BBR was checked in eight exploratory configurations and lies outside the model. The link between the stack and financial deployments is an assumption of scope: gateways and API services that terminate TLS over TCP are exposed to the same two mechanisms, but we did not measure whether Linux/OpenSSL represents the Java, appliance or mainframe stacks of financial networks. On another stack the round trips may fall at other flights; the model needs only the write pattern of the library and the window rule of the kernel to be replaced.

The testbed and the model use full handshakes without session tickets, whereas production servers issue them. In a follow-up experiment, a full transaction that also issued one ticket was half a round trip slower in 6 of 40 configurations (only at 30 and 100\ms; at 5\ms the difference is at most 1.2\ms) and equal in the others (\cref{S-tab:ticket}). The latencies reported here may therefore be up to half a round trip too low where tickets are issued, and the model has no mode for it. In captures, the ticket is a separate write issued when the client's Finished arrives. We tested the model with the ticket as an extra server write, joined with the response or issued on the Finished: neither reproduces the effect (the model changes by $+0.1$ to $+0.5$\ms in the six slow configurations, against $+15$ and $+50$\ms measured; \cref{S-app:extra}). The cause is therefore not established, and the results of this paper apply to full handshakes without tickets.\par The CPU clock was fixed (\texttt{performance} governor, turbo disabled) for the identification and validation campaigns (\cref{sec:testbed}); only the first session of the runs with other TCP settings used the \texttt{ondemand} governor with turbo enabled. Frequency changes are therefore unlikely to explain the residual of the validation sets. Subtraction of unshaped medians reduces shared local processing costs. It cannot guarantee the cancellation of scheduling or network--computation overlap. Only 46 of 226 model predictions lie inside the measured 95\% bootstrap interval. The low MAE does not indicate agreement within measurement uncertainty.

The byte and CPU inputs are measured for the supported components. Falcon, SLH-DSA, and composite configurations did not run as full TLS transactions. Protocol message sizes and the default XMLDSig certificate embedding are scenario choices. The model writes each application message at once, whereas OpenSSL writes a message above 16\,KB as one write per 16\,KB record; all validated messages are shorter (Supplementary Appendix~\ref{S-app:records}). The main sweep gives both mTLS endpoints the same chain depth (\cref{S-tab:asym} varies them separately), and chains in which a certificate of one algorithm is signed by a CA of another algorithm are modeled only in one scenario without measurement (\cref{S-tab:mixed}). $\bar{\Delta}_d$ measures an average simultaneous depth increment that adds a certificate at each endpoint. It does not measure the marginal delay of one certificate.

A direct measurement of an event-driven OpenSSL~3.5.4 server (\cref{S-tab:srvcpu}) puts the CPU per full handshake at 1.18--1.35 times the primitive costs of the model for the post-quantum and RSA suites and at 1.9--2.8 times for the classical ECDSA suites, within the CPU scales of 1 to 16 swept here. For resumed handshakes the ratio is 4.5--19.8, at 0.18--0.21\ms per transaction. The excess over the primitives is 0.09--0.24\ms in every row, resumed handshakes included, while the primitives range from 0.009 to 1.16\ms: the large ratios of resumed handshakes come from a small denominator, a per-connection cost of the server that the primitive costs of the model do not include (\cref{S-tab:srvcpu}). No HSM, middleware, queue, database or backend payment process was measured. Project Leap Phase~2 reports receiver-side verification of 209.9\ms for its integrated Dilithium application~\cite{bis_leap2025}, much larger than the primitive costs here; its processing scope and algorithms differ. The finding that size-dependent transport overhead exceeds the modeled primitive time applies to the modeled operations only. It does not apply to all processing behind a payment gateway, and the order of the signature schemes on stacks that we did not test is not established.

HAProxy is the only measured intermediary. Its sub-millisecond added delay does not validate nonzero threshold delays for deep packet inspection, TLS termination or hardware firewalls. Packet loss, receive-window limits (at an MTU of 9\,000\,bytes the default receive buffer limits a flight to seven segments; the validation does not cover this MTU, \cref{sec:testbed}), wide-area jitter, competing traffic and operational tail latency remain unvalidated. Bit rates above 1\,000\Mbits and RTTs outside 5, 30 and 100\ms are model extrapolations. The 90\% uncertainty intervals are conditional on assumed input distributions (\cref{sec:rq5}); they are not confidence intervals for a population of financial deployments.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Post-quantum authentication changes transport latency when certificate and application-signature bytes move a flight across the available TCP window. A model that incorporates the observed OpenSSL write pattern and Linux-style window validation locates these crossings on the tested stack. It predicts configuration-level excess latency with an MAE of 1.65\ms across 226 testbed configurations; the idealized model gives 4.70\ms. The later off-grid configurations yield an MAE of 2.04\ms, and 46 of 226 predictions fall within the measured 95\% bootstrap interval. The model retains a systematic residual of a few milliseconds, and the validation does not cover an MTU of 9\,000 bytes.

With zero added middlebox delay, size-dependent transport overhead exceeds the modeled cryptographic-primitive time in 75.6\% of the equally weighted ML-DSA-65 cold grid. In the reference SWIFT~MX scenario, increasing the two ML-DSA-65 chain depths from 1 to 4 adds 60.0\ms, and slow-start waiting contributes 56.5\ms. The depth cost depends on the initial window and is not a constant per certificate. The mTLS chains and the application signatures each contribute to the latency overhead. Connection reuse, reduced certificate embedding and suitable window settings avoid some RTT steps, whereas zlib compression helps little. These results concern the selected profiles and the modeled operations. Future work will validate the model on other TLS stacks, wide-area paths and operational payment gateways.
